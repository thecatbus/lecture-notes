This is a fairly gentle introduction to the representation theory of the
symmetric group. The flavour will be both algebraic and combinatorial, in
particular a first course on representation theory is prerequisite.

Why study representations of symmetric groups? Firstly it is a nice theory. For
instance, there is a \emph{canonical} classification of the irreducibles ---
irreducible representations of $\sym{n}$ over $\bbC$ are parametrised by
\emph{partitions} of $n$. The construction works uniformly for all symmetric
groups, much unlike the case of general finite groups where multiple ad hoc
constructions are needed for each case. 

Another reason is that the theory of symmetric groups serves as fodder for the
theory for other algebras that are diagrammatic in flavour, such as (pure) braid
groups. 

\paragraph{Schur--Weyl duality.} Here is a third reason the representation theory
of the symmetric group is nice. For $V$ a $\bbC$-vector space, the tensor power
$V^{\otimes 2}$ is a representation of $\mathsf{GL}(V)\times \sym{2}$ where
$\mathsf{GL}(V)$ acts on each factor, and $\sym{2}$ acts by permuting factors
(i.e.\ $v\otimes w\mapsto w\otimes v$). Then $V^{\otimes 2}$ splits into
irreducible representations as $\Sym^2 V \oplus \Exter^2 V$; there exists a
correspondence between representations of $\sym{2}$ (namely the trivial and sign
representation) and those of $\mathsf{GL}(V)$ (the two factors of $V^{\otimes
2}$ respectively). \emph{Schur--Weyl duality} extends this theory from $\sym{2}$
to $\sym{r}$. That is, consider the representation $V^{\otimes r}$ of
$\GL{V}\times \sym{r}$ where $\GL{V}$ acts on each factor and $\sym{r}$ permutes
the factors. Then Schur--Weyl duality shows $V^{\otimes r}=\bigoplus_i
A_i\otimes B_i$ where $\{A_i\}$ are irreducible representations of $\GL{V}$ and
$\{B_i\}$ are irreducible representations of $\sym{r}$. Again, there is a
correspondence $A_i\leftrightarrow B_i$ between some representations of $\GL{V}$
and some representations of $\sym{r}$.

Alas this correspondence is not usually bijective --- one issue is that $\GL{V}$
has infinitely many irreducible representations while $\sym{r}$ has a finite
number has a finite number. Another problem is taking inverse powers of
determinants. 

But the correspondence is \emph{almost} bijective if we stabilise ---  if we let
$\dim(V)$ be large then we recover all representations of $\sym{r}$, while if we
let $r$ be large then we recover all representations of $\GL{V}$ up to powers of
the determinant. We won't look at this.

\paragraph{Topology.} A fourth reason comes by taking any topological space $X$
and considering its \emph{configuration space} $\sf{C}^nX$ (i.e.\ the moduli of
$n$ distinct unordered points in $X$). The group $\sym{n}$ naturally acts on
$\sf{C}^n X$, giving a representation of $\sym{n}$ on $\HH^i(\sf{C}^nX, \bbC)$.

\paragraph{In positive characteristic.} Representations over fields of
prime characteristic can be constructed in a `similar' manner to the
characteristic zero theory, which is again unlike the general case for finite
groups (modular representation theory tends to be much, much harder in
prime characteristic than over $\bbC$; even constructing characters is hard).

\section{Representations and modules}
\marginpar{Lecture 1, 12/10/26} 
We go over some standard results in the representation theory of a finite group
$G$ over a field $\bbk$. By \emph{module}, we will always mean finite
dimensional left modules.

\begin{definition} 
    A \emph{representation} of $G$ on a finite dimensional $\bbk$-vector space
    $V$ is any (and all) of the following.
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item a homomorphism $\rho\colon G\to \GL{V}$;
        \item a linear action of $G$ on $V$, i.e.\ a map $G\times V \to V$ such
            that for all $g,h\in G$ and $v,w\in V$ we have $(gh)(v)=g(h(v))$,
            $1(v)=v$, $g(\alpha v + \beta w)=\alpha(gv)+\beta(gw)$;
        \item a $\bbk G$-module structure on $V$, where $\bbk G$ is the
            \emph{group algebra} (the free $\bbk$-module with basis $G$, with
            product given by extending the multiplication in $G$ linearly). 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    The one-dimensional trivial representation of $G$, denoted $\mathbf{1}_G$, is
    given by $V=\bbk$ with $g(v)=v$ for all $g\in G$, $v\in V$.
\end{example}

If $W$ is a subrepresentation of $V$ (i.e.\ a $\bbk G$-submodule) then we write
$W\leq V$. A representation $V$ is \emph{simple} (or \emph{irreducible}) if it
is a simple $\bbk G$-module, i.e.\ it is non-zero and its only
subrepresentations are $\mathbf{0}$ and $V$. We say $V$ is \emph{semisimple} (or
\emph{complete reducible}) if it is a direct sum of simple representations (this
property is closed under taking sub-objects and quotients). We say $V$ is
\emph{indecomposable} if it is not a direct sum of proper subrepresentations, we
say it is \emph{decomposable} otherwise. 

\begin{theorem}[(Maschke)]
    If $\bbk$ has characteristic zero, then every subrepresentation of $V$ is in
    fact a direct summand. In other words, irreducible and indecomposable
    representations of $V$ coincide.
\end{theorem}

A \emph{$G$-homomorphism} between two $\bbk G$-modules $V,W$ is a $\bbk G$-linear
map $\alpha\colon V\to W$.

Suppose $\{L_i\}$ is a collection of pairwise non-isomorphic irreducible
representations of $G$. Suppose $V$ is semisimple. Then there is an isomorphism
$f\colon \bigoplus L_i ^{\oplus n_i}\to V$ for some non-negative integers $n_i$. The
isomorphism is not canonical; but the embedding $f(L_i^{\oplus n_i})\into V$
\emph{is canonical} --- it is the sum of the images of all maps $L_i\to V$,
called the \emph{$L_i$-isotypic piece}. Thus each $\bbk G$-module $V$ is
\emph{canonically} isomorphic to the direct sum of its isotypic components, and
each $L_i$-isotypic component admits a non-canonical decomposition into a direct sum
of $L_i$s.

\begin{theorem}[(Schur's lemma)]
    Suppose $V,W$ are irreducible representations of $G$. Any $G$-homomorphism
    $V\to W$ is either zero or is an isomorphism. In particular, $\End_GV$ is
    a division algebra over $\bbk$ (if $\bbk$ is algebraically closed then
    $\End_GV\simeq \bbk$).
\end{theorem}

\marginpar{Lecture 2, 14/10/26} 
\begin{definition}
    For $G$-representations $L_i, V$ such that $L_i$ is irreducible, the \emph{$L_i$-multiplicity space} in $V$ is $\Hom_G(L_i, V)=M_i$.
\end{definition}

Suppose $\bbk=\ol\bbk$ is algebraically closed, and $V$ is a finite dimensional
isotypic representation of $G$ (that is, $V\simeq L_i^{\oplus n_i}$ for some
irreducible representation $L_i$). In this case, the canonical map $\Hom_G(L_i,
V)\otimes L_i\to V$ is an isomorphism. If $V$ is semisimple and $\{M_i\}$ are the
multiplicity spaces of irreducible representations $\{L_i\}$, then the
analogously defined map $\bigoplus M_i\otimes L_i \to V$ is an isomorphism. If
$V$ is not semisimple, then this map is only injective (and the image of the map
is called the \emph{socle} of $V$).

\begin{proposition}
    The left regular representation of $G$, given by $V=\bbk G$ where $G$ acts by left
    multiplication, is semisimple and has a $(\dim L_i)$-dimensional
    multiplicity space for each irreducible representation $L_i$. In other words
    it decomposes as \[V=\bigoplus_{L_i\in \textnormal{Irr}(G)} L_i^{\oplus \dim L_i}\]
    with corollary that $|G|=\sum (\dim L_i)^2$.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Maschke's theorem again.} Call a $\bbk G$-module $P$
\emph{projective} if every short exact sequence of $\bbk G$-modules $0\to N\to
N'\xrightarrow{\alpha} P\to 0$ splits, i.e.\ there exists a map $\beta\colon
P\to N'$ with $\alpha\beta = \id_P$. Every representation of $\bbk G$ is
semisimple if and only if the trivial representation $\bbOne_G$ is projective.
Then Maschke's theorem states that if $G$ is a finite group and the
characteristic of $\bbk$ does not divide $|G|$, then $\bbOne_G$ is projective
and hence every representation of $G$ is semisimple.

\begin{remark}
    If the characteristic of $\bbk$ divides $|G|$, then this \emph{always}
    fails. For example take $G=\bbZ/p$ and $\bbk = \bbF_p$, and consider the
    representation $V=\bbF_p^{\oplus 2}$ where $a\in G$ acts as
    $\mat{1&a\\0&1}$. Then $V$ is a nontrivial self-extension of the trivial
    representation $\bbOne_G$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Tensor products.} The \emph{tensor product} $V\otimes_\bbk
W$ of two $G$-representations $V,W$ has the structure of a $G$-module given by
$g(v\otimes w)=(gv) \otimes (gw)$. 

\paragraph{Induction and Restriction.} For $H\leq G$, there is a
\emph{restriction} functor $\text{Res}^G_H\colon \text{Rep}(G)\to \text{Rep}(H)$
where the $\bbk G$ module $V$ is viewed as a $\bbk H$-module in the obvious way.
We sometimes denote the restricted module as $\text{Res}^G_H V =
V\!\downarrow^G_H = V\!\downarrow_H$. It has a left adjoint, the \emph{induction
functor} $\text{Ind}^G_H\colon \text{Rep}(H)\to \text{Rep}(G)$ given by
$\text{Ind}^G_H(-)=\bbk G\otimes_{\bbk H }(-)$. We then have \emph{Frobenius
reciprocity}, \[\Hom_G(\text{Ind}^G_H U, V)= \Hom_H(U, \text{Res}^G_H V).\]

\paragraph{Permutation modules.} A \emph{permutation module} is a representation
of $G$ equipped with a basis on which $G$ acts, i.e.\ a basis $B$ such that
$gb\in B$ for all $g\in G$ and $b\in B$. In this case we denote the module
$\bbk[B]$. For example, the left regular
representation is naturally a permutation module.

\begin{proposition}
    If $G$ acts transitively on a finite set $X$, then the corresponding
    permutation module $\bbk [X]$ is isomorphic to the induced representation
    $\textnormal{Ind}_H^G(\bbOne_H)$ where $H\leq G$ is the stabiliser of any point
    $x\in X$, and $\bbOne_H$ is the trivial representation.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Duals.} If $V$ is a finite dimensional
$\bbk$-vector space, then so is $V\dual = \Hom_\bbk (V,\bbk)$. If $e_1,\ldots,
e_n$ is a basis of $V$ then $V\dual$ is naturally equipped with a dual basis
$\varepsilon_1,\ldots, \varepsilon_n$ such that
$\varepsilon_i(e_j)=\delta_{ij}$. If $V$ is a $G$-representation, then so is $V\dual$ via $g\varphi = \varphi \circ g^{-1}$.

\paragraph{Some linear algebra.}
Suppose $V$ is equipped with a symmetric bilinear form $\langle \cdot, \cdot
\rangle$. This induces a natural map $V\to V\dual$ given by $v\mapsto \langle
\cdot, v\rangle$. Even if $\langle \cdot,\cdot \rangle$ is non-singular (i.e.\
$V\orth = 0$)  we could still have a subspace $W\subset V$ with $W\orth \cap W
\neq 0 $. 

So we will be interested in how big such intersections can get. 

Let $e_1,\ldots, e_n$ be a basis of the subspace $W$. The \emph{Gram matrix} of
$W$ with respect to this basis is the $n\times n$ matrix $A$ with $(i,j)$th
entry $\langle e_i, e_j\rangle$.

\begin{proposition}
    The dimension of $W/(W\cap W\orth)$ is the rank of the Gram matrix.

    \begin{proof}
        The induced map $W\to W\dual$ given by $w\mapsto \langle \cdot,
        w\rangle$ is precisely the map given by the Gram matrix when we equip
        $W, W\dual$ with dual bases. The kernel of the map is $W\cap W\orth$, so
        the answer follows from rank--nullity.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Character theory.} Work over the field $\bbk = \bbC$, and let $V$ be
a finite dimensional $\bbC G$-module. The \emph{character} of $V$ is a function
$\chi_V\colon G\to \bbC$ given by $g\mapsto \text{tr}(g\colon V\to
V)$. This is a \emph{class function}, i.e.\ it is invariant under conjugation.
Some useful calculations --- 
\begin{gather*}
    \chi_{(V\oplus W)} = \chi_V + \chi_W, \qquad \chi_{(V\dual)} = \ol{\chi_V},
    \qquad \chi_{(V\otimes W)} = \chi_V \cdot \chi_W, \qquad \chi_{\Hom(V,W)} =
    \ol{\chi_V}\cdot \chi_W, \\ 
    \text{for the permutation module determined by }G\circlearrowright B, \qquad
    \chi_{\bbk[B]}(g) =\#\{b\in B\;|\; gb = b\}.
\end{gather*}

Character theory is most useful because of the following result. 

\begin{proposition}
    The space of class functions on $G$ has basis given by the characters of
    irreducible representations.
\end{proposition}

In positive characteristic we need to use \emph{Brauer characters}.

\section{Representations of the symmetric groups}
\marginpar{Lecture 3, 19/10/26} 
We now overview the combinatorial approach to representation theory of the
symmetric groups, via a certain \emph{diagram calculus}. Thus for a finite set
$X$, write $\Sym(X)=\{\text{bijections }X\to X\}$ for the \emph{symmetric group
of $X$}. In particular, the standard symmetric groups $\sym{n}$ are the same as
$\Sym([n])$ where $[n]$ denotes the set $\{1,\ldots, n\}$. Note also that
$\sym{0}$ is the trivial group.

While we are establishing conventions, we also note that composition goes from
right to left: $(1\,2\,3)(1\,2)=(1\,3)$.

\paragraph{Representations we have already met.}
The group $\sym{n}$, like any other group, has the trivial representation which
we denote $\bbOne_{\sym{n}}$. There is only one other one-dimensional
representation, the \emph{alternating} or \emph{sign} representation, which we
denote $\sgn\colon \sym{n}\to \{\pm 1\}$.

There is also the natural representation $\sym{n}\circlearrowright \bbC^n$ given
by permuting the standard basis $\{e_1,\ldots, e_n\}$, called the \emph{natural} or the
\emph{permutation representation}. We write $V_n$ for this representation. Note
that the element $(e_1+\cdots + e_n)\in V_n$ is $\sym{n}$-invariant, so $V_n$ is
never irreducible (unless $n=1$).

We make the following observations about the natural representations $V_n$: 
\begin{gather*}
    V_n = (\bbOne_{\sym{n-1}})\uparrow^{\sym{n}}_{\sym{n-1}}\,, \qquad V_n
    \downarrow^{\sym{n}}_{\sym{n-1}} = V_{n-1} \oplus \bbOne_{\sym{n-1}}.
\end{gather*}

\begin{exercise}
    Define the \emph{augmentation map} $\varepsilon\colon V_n\to \bbC$ by
    $e_1\mapsto 1$. Show that if $n>1$, then $\ker(\varepsilon)$ is an irreducible
    $(n-1)$-dimensional $\sym{n}$-subrepresentation of $V_n$. We call this the
    \emph{standard representation of $\sym{n}$}.
\end{exercise}

\paragraph{Partitions.} Now recall that every element $\sigma\in \sym{n}$ can be
decomposed as a product $\sigma = \sigma_1\cdots \sigma_k$ of disjoint cycles
(in a way that every $i\in [n]$ appears in exactly one cycle).

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{partition $\lambda$ of $n$}, written $\lambda \vdash n$, is a
    non-increasing sequence of positive integers summing to $n$. That is,
    $\lambda$ is given by a tuple \(\lambda = (\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)\) of
    integers \(\lambda_1\geq \cdots \geq \lambda_r> 0\), satisfying
    \(\sum_{i=1}^r\lambda_i = n\). In this case we say the $\lambda_i$s are the \emph{parts} of $\lambda$, $n=|\lambda|$ is
    the \emph{size} of $\lambda$, and $r=\ell(\lambda)$ is the \emph{length} of
    $\lambda$. 

    Write $\calP(n)=\{\lambda\;|\; \lambda\vdash n\}$ for the set of all
    partitions of $n$.
\end{definition}

We adopt the convention that the length-zero tuple $\varnothing = ()$ is a partition
of $0$, called the \emph{empty partition}. We also adopt a condensed notation
that collapses equal parts together, for example we write $(4,3^2,1)$ for the
partition $(4,3,3,1)\vdash 11$.

Note that partitions of $n$ are naturally in bijection with cycle types, and
thus conjugacy classes in $\sym{n}$. For example, the three partitions $(3)$,
$(2,1)$, and $(1^3)$ corresponds to the three classes (3-cycles, transpositions,
and identity) in $\sym{3}$.

\begin{definition} The \emph{Young diagram} of a partition
    $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ is 
$[\lambda] = \{(i,j)\in \bbN^2\;|\; 1\leq i\leq r, \quad 1\leq j \leq
\lambda_i\}$.
\end{definition}

We draw Young diagrams with boxes (instead of lattice points), with rows and
columns numbered as in a matrix. For example, here are all the partitions of
$4$ and their Young diagrams.
\[
    {
    \setlength{\arraycolsep}{2em}
    \begin{array}{ccccc}
        (4) & (3,1) & (2^2) & (2,1^2) & (1^4)\\
        \ydiagram{4} & \ydiagram{3,1} & \ydiagram{2,2} &\ydiagram{2,1,1}
                     &\ydiagram{1,1,1,1}
    \end{array}
    }
\]

\begin{definition} 
    Given $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)\vdash n$, the
    \emph{conjugate partition} $\lambda'$ is given by $\lambda'=(\mu_1,\ldots,
    \mu_{\lambda_1})$, where $\mu_j = \{i\in [r]\;|\; \lambda_i \geq j\}\). That
    is, $\lambda'$ is the partition whose Young diagram contains $(i,j)$ if and
    only if $(j,i)\in [\lambda]$.
\end{definition}

Thus pictorially, conjugation corresponds to flipping Young diagrams along the
diagonal. For example, $(4,3,1)$ and
$(3,2^2,1)$ are conjugate partitions of $8$. 
\[
    \ydiagram{4,3,1} \qquad \ydiagram{3,2,2,1}
\]


\begin{definition}
    Let $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ and $\mu=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_s)$ be
    partitions of $n\in \bbN$. We say $\lambda$ \emph{dominates} $\mu$, written
    $\lambda \unrhd \mu$, if $\sum_1^j\lambda_i\geq \sum_1^j\mu_i$ for all
    $j\in\{1,\ldots,\min(r,s)\}.$
\end{definition}

This is a partial order on $\calP(n)$, for example $(4,3,1)$ and
$(5,1^3)$ are incomparable in $\calP(8)$. Below is the Hasse diagram for
$\calP(6)$.
\[
    \begin{tikzcd}[column sep=small]
        &&& (4,1^2)\arrow[dr] && (3,1^3)\arrow[dr] &&& \\
        (6)\rar & (5,1)\rar & (4,2)\arrow[ur]\arrow[dr] && (3,2,1)\arrow[ur]\arrow[dr] && (2^2,1^2)\rar & (2,1^4)\rar & (1^6)\\
        &&& (3,3)\arrow[ur] && (2^3)\arrow[ur] &&&
    \end{tikzcd}
\]

There is also the \emph{lexicographic
order} on $\calP(n)$, where for distinct partitions
$\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ and $\mu=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_r)$ of $n$ we
say $\lambda>\mu$ if and only if there exists an $i\in \{1,\ldots, \min(r,s)\}$
such that $\lambda_i>\mu_i$, and $\lambda_j = \mu_j$ for $j=1,\ldots, i-1$. It
is easy to see that the lexicographic order \emph{extends} the domination order,
i.e.\ if $\lambda\unrhd \mu$ then $\lambda \geq \mu$. The converse is false,
since the lexicographic order is always a total order --- for example, the order
on $\calP(6)$ is given by
\[(6)\;>\;(5,1)\;>\;(4,2)\;>\;(4,1^2)\;>\;(3^2)\;>\;(3,2,1)\;>\;(3,1^3)\;>\;(2^3)\;>\;(2^2,1^2)\;>\;(2,1^4)\;>\;(1^6).\]

\begin{definition} 
    Given a partition $\lambda\vdash n$, a \emph{$\lambda$-tableau} (or a
    \emph{Young tableau of shape $\lambda$}) is a bijection $[\lambda]\to [n]$.
    We write $\Delta^\lambda$ for the set of all $\lambda$-tableaux.
\end{definition}

A $\lambda$-tableau can be naturally denoted by `filling in' the Young diagram
with numbers $1,\ldots, n$ such that each number appears once. For example, here
are all $(3,1)$-tableaux, i.e.\ the set $\Delta^{(3,1)}$.

\begin{definition}
    Let $\lambda$ be a partition of $n$. The \emph{Specht module} $S^\lambda$ is
    the submodule \(\langle e_t\;|\; t\in \Delta^\lambda \rangle \subset
    M^\lambda\) spanned by polytabloids corresponding to tableaux of shape
    $\lambda$.
\end{definition}

It is easy to check that $S^\lambda$ is indeed a module for $\sym{n}$. We have
the following properties.

\begin{lemma}
    Let $\lambda$ be a partition of $n$, and $t\in \Delta^\lambda$. Then we have
    the following. 
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item\label{item:specht1} $e_t\neq 0$.
        \item\label{item:specht2} $\forall \sigma\in\sym{n}$, $\sigma\cdot e_t = e_{\sigma t}$.
        \item\label{item:specht3} $\forall \sigma\in C_t$, $\sigma\cdot e_t =
            \textnormal{sgn}(\sigma)e_t$.
        \item\label{item:specht4} $S^\lambda$ is a \emph{cyclic} submodule of
            $M^\lambda$, i.e.\ $S^\lambda=\bbk \sym{n}\cdot e_t$ for some
            (equivalently any) $t\in \Delta^\lambda$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \begin{proof}
        To prove~\ref{item:specht1}, note that $R_t\cap C_t=\{1\}$ and hence if
        $\sigma\in C_t$, then $\sigma\{t\} = \{t\}$ implies $\sigma=1$. The
        coefficient of the tabloid $\{t\}$ in $e_t$ is
        $\textnormal{sgn}(1)=1\neq 0$, so $e_t$ is non-zero.
        
        Now it is clear that $C_{\sigma t} = \sigma C_t \sigma^{-1}$. Thus
        \[
            \sigma e_t = \sigma \sum_{\tau\in C_t} (\textnormal{sgn}\tau)\cdot
            \tau\{t\} = \sum_{\tau\in C_t}(\textnormal{sgn}\tau)\cdot
            \sigma\tau\{t\} = \sum_{\tau \in
            C_t}(\textnormal{sgn}(\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}))\cdot
            (\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1})\{\sigma t\} = \sum_{\omega\in C_{\sigma
            t}}(\textnormal{sgn}\omega)\cot \omega\{\sigma t\} = e_{\sigma t}
        \] 
        thus proving~\ref{item:specht2}.

        To prove~\ref{item:specht3}, note that if $\sigma\in C_t$ then 
        \[
            \sigma\cdot e_t = \sum_{\tau\in
            C_t}(\textnormal{sgn}\tau)\{\sigma\tau\cdot t\} = \sum_{\omega\in C_t}
            (\textnormal{sgn}(\sigma^{-1}\omega))\{\omega t\} =
            (\textnormal{sgn}\sigma) e_t.
        \]

        Lastly, to show that $S^\lambda$ is cyclic we note
        that $\sym{n}$ acts transitively on $\Delta^\lambda$, so
        that~\ref{item:specht2} allows the conclusion.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\end{exercise}

\begin{example}

\end{example}

We now prove that over the field $\bbC$, the Specht modules
$\{S^\lambda\;|\;\lambda\vdash n\}$ form a complete set of irreducible
$\bbC\sym{n}$-modules.

\begin{definition}
    For a partition $\lambda\vdash n$, the module $M^\lambda$ has a
    $\sym{n}$-invariant symmetric bilinear inner product $\langle\cdot, \cdot
    \rangle$ defined by $\langle\, \{t\}, \{t'\}\,\rangle = \delta_{\{t\}\{t'\}}$
    for $t,t'\in \Delta^\lambda$, and extended linearly. The \emph{orthogonal
    complement} $N\orth$ of a subspace $N\subset M^\lambda$ is the subspace
    $\{m\in M^\lambda\;|\; \langle m,n\rangle = 0 \text{ for all }n\in N\}$.
\end{definition}

Note that if $N$ is a $\sym{n}$-submodule of $M^\lambda$ then so is $N\orth$,
since the inner product is $\sym{n}$-invariant. 

\begin{theorem}[(James' submodule theorem)]
    If $\lambda \vdash n$ and $V\leq M^\lambda$ is a
    $\sym{n}$-subrepresentation, then either $S^\lambda\subseteq V$ or
    $V\subseteq (S^{\lambda})\orth$. In particular, quotients of $S^\lambda$ are
    simple.
\end{theorem}

This will imply that $S^\lambda$ is irreducible over a field of characteristic
zero. To show the $S^\lambda$s are pairwise non-isomorphic is a bit harder.

A first step to proving James' theorem is the following.

\begin{proposition}
    \marginpar{Lecture 6, 28/10/26} 
    Let $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots, \lambda_r)$ be a partition of $n$, and
    $t,u\in \Delta^\lambda$ such that $\kappa_t\{u\}\neq 0$.
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item\label{item:tech1} $\exists h\in C_t$ such that $h\{t\}=\{u\}$,
        \item\label{item:tech2} $\kappa_t\{u\}=\pm e_t$,
        \item\label{item:tech3} $\kappa_t M^\lambda = \bbk e_t$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \begin{proof}
        To show~\ref{item:tech1}, we construct $h\in C_t$ such that
        $R_i(ht)=R_i(u)$ for all $i$. 

        Claim that if $\kappa_t(u)\neq 0$, then any
        numbers $x\neq y$ appearing in the same row of $u$ must appear in
        different columns of $t$. If not, then the transposition $(x\,y)$ lies
        in $C_t$. Let $\mathcal{T}$ be a set of (left) coset representatives of
        $\langle (x\,y)\rangle$ in $C_t$. That is, $C_t=\mathcal{T}\cdot \id
        \;\cup\; \mathcal{T}\cdot (x\,y)$. Then 
        \[\kappa_t = \sum_{\sigma\in C_t}(\sgn\sigma)\sigma = \sum_{t\in
        \mathcal{T}}(\sgn\,t)t(1-(x\,y))\]
        and hence
        $\kappa_t\{u\}=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}(\sgn\,t)t(\{u\}-(x\,y)\{u\})$
        which vanishes since $(x\,y)\in R_u$ (since $x,y$ lie in the same row in
        $u$). This proves the claim.

        Now suppose $R_1(u)=\{x_1,\ldots,x_{\lambda_1}\}$ and that $x_s$ belongs
        to column $j_s$ of $t$, for each $s\in [\lambda_1]$. The $j_s$s are
        pairwise distinct by the above claim. Let $y_s=t((1,j_s))$, and define $h_1=\prod_{\substack{s\in[\lambda_1]\\x_s\neq y_s}} (x_s\,y_s)$. 

        Note $R_1(h_1 t)=\{h_1y_1, \ldots, h_1y_{\lambda_1}\} =
        \{x_1,\ldots,x_{\lambda_1}\}=R_1(u)$. Since $h_1\in C_t$, we see that
        $C_{h_1t}=h_1C_th_1^{-1}=C_t$, and hence $\kappa_t=\kappa_{h_1t}$ does
        not vanish on $\{u\}$.

        Continuing, suppose $R_2(u)=\{x_1',\ldots, x_{\lambda_2}'\}$ with $x_s'$
        belonging to column $j_s'$ of $t'=h_1t$. By the claim, the $j_s$s are pairwise
        distinct. Again, let $y_s'=t((2,j_s'))$ and define
        $h_2=\prod_{\substack{s\in[\lambda_2]\\ x_s'\neq y_s'}}(x_s'\,y_s')$.

        This lies in $C_{t'}=C_t$, and $R_2(h_2\,t')=R_2(u)$ and
        $R_1(h_2t')=R_1(t')=R_1(u)$. In conclusion, $R_i(h_2h_1t)=R_i(u)$ for
        both $i=1,2$. Iterating, we construct for each $m\in \{3,4,\ldots, r\}$
        an element $h_m\in C_t$ such that $R_i(h_mh_{m-1}\ldots h_1 t)=R_i(u)$
        for all $i\in [m]$. Declaring $h=h_r\cdots h_1$, we have $h\{t\}=\{u\}$
        as required.

        To show~\ref{item:tech2}, take $h$ as above and note that
        $\kappa_t\{u\}=\kappa_t h\{t\}=(\sgn\,h)\kappa_t\{t\} = (\sgn\,h)e_t$,
        which is $\pm e_t$ as required. Then~\ref{item:tech3} follows, since
        for each basis element $\{u\}\in M^\lambda$ we either have
        $\kappa_t\{u\}=0$ or $\kappa_t\{u\}=\pm e_t$. Thus $\kappa_t
        M^\lambda\subseteq\bbk e_t$, and equality holds since
        $\kappa_t\{t\}=e_t$.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of James' submodule theorem]
    Let $\lambda\vdash n$ and $V\leq M^\lambda$ be as given, and assume
    $V\not\leq (S^\lambda)\orth$. Then there is a $v\in V$ and $t\in
    \Delta^\lambda$ such that $\langle v, e^t\rangle\neq 0$. Thus $\langle v,
    e_t\rangle = \sum_{\sigma\in C_t} (\sgn \sigma)\langle \sigma^{-1}v,
    \{t\}\rangle = \langle \kappa_t v, \{t\}\rangle$ is non-zero.

    In particular, $\kappa_t v\neq 0$, so $\kappa_t v = \beta e_t$ for some
    $\beta\in \bbk^\times$. Since $\kappa_t v\in V$, it follows that $e_t\in V$
    and thus $S^\lambda = \bbk \sym{n} \cdot e_t \leq V$ as required.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    If $\lambda \vdash n$ then $S^\lambda/(S^\lambda \cap (S^\lambda) \orth)$ is
    either zero or irreducible.

    \begin{proof}
        If $S^\lambda\leq (S^\lambda)\orth$ then the quotient is zero.
        Otherwise, take any submodule $V\leq S^\lambda$. By James' submodule theorem,
        $V=S^\lambda$ or $V\leq S^\lambda \cap (S^\lambda)\orth$. That is,
        $S^\lambda\cap (S^\lambda)\orth$ is a maximal submodule of $S^\lambda$,
        so the quotient is irreducible.
    \end{proof}
\end{corollary}

\begin{definition}
    Given $\lambda \vdash n$, the \emph{Young module} $Y^\lambda$ is the unique
    indecomposable summand of $M^\lambda$ containing the Specht module $S^\lambda$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    A representation $\rho\colon G\to \mathsf{GL}_n(\bbk)$ is \emph{absolutely
    irreducible} if, for any field extension $\bbk\subseteq \bbK$, the
    corresponding representation $\ol\rho\colon G\to \mathsf{GL}_n(\bbK)$ is
    irreducible.
\end{definition}

\begin{example} 
    Consider the cyclic group $C_4=\langle \alpha \rangle$, which has a two-dimensional
    $\bbQ$-representation given by $\alpha \mapsto \mat{0&1 \\ -1 &
    0}$. This representation is irreducible. However, upon extension to
    $\bbQ(\sqrt{-1})$, the generator $\ol\rho(\alpha)$ has eigenvalues $\pm
    \sqrt{-1}$ and hence the representation splits. 
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
    \marginpar{Lecture 7, 2/11/26} 
    If $\lambda \vdash n$ then $S^\lambda/(S^\lambda \cap (S^\lambda) \orth)$ is
    either zero or \emph{absolutely} irreducible.

    \begin{proof}
        Choose a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ of polytabloids in $S^\lambda$. Note that
        $\dim(S^\lambda/S^\lambda\cap (S^\lambda)\orth)$ is the rank of the Gram
        matrix $A$ taken with respect to this basis. But the entries
        $A_{ij}=\langle e_i, e_j\rangle$ all lie in the prime subfield of $\bbk$
        (i.e.\ $\bbF_p$ or $\bbQ$) so the rank doesn't change upon
        extending $\bbk$. Since we have already shown $S^\lambda/(S^\lambda\cap
        (S^\lambda)\orth)$ is zero or irreducible, the result follows.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}
    If $\textnormal{char}(\bbk)=0$ then $S_\bbk^\lambda$ is irreducible for all
    $\lambda$.

    \begin{proof}
        Over $\bbQ$, the bilinear form $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ is
        positive definite on $M^\lambda$. So $S^\lambda_\bbQ\cap
        (S^\lambda_\bbQ)\orth = 0$. Thus $S^\lambda_\bbQ$ is absolutely
        irreducible, whence $S^\lambda_\bbk$ is irreducible since any field
        $\bbk$ of characteristic zero extends $\bbQ$.
    \end{proof}
\end{corollary}

Is $\{S^\lambda\;|\; \lambda\vdash n\}$ a complete set of irreducible
representations?

\begin{proposition}
    Suppose $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ and
    $\mu=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_s)$ are partitions of $n$. Take $t\in
    \Delta^\lambda$. Choose $u\in \Delta^\mu$ such that $\kappa_t\{u\}\neq 0$. Then 
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item\label{item:3.10.1}
            there is an $h\in C_t$ such that for all $\ell\in
            \{1,2,\ldots,\min(r,s)\}$ we have $\bigsqcup_{i=1}^\ell
            R_i(u)\subseteq \bigsqcup_{i=1}^\ell R_i(ht)$, and
        \item\label{item:3.10.2}
            we have $\lambda \trianglerighteq \mu$ in the dominance order.
    \end{enumerate}

    \begin{proof}
        Claim that if $x\neq y$ appear in the same row of $u$ then
        they are in different columns of $t$; this is proved similarly as
        before. Given this, suppose $R_1(u)=\{x_1,\ldots, x_r\}$ and that $x_q$
        lies in column $j_q$ of $t$. By the claim, $j_q$s are pairwise distinct.
        Let $y_q=t((1,j_q))$ and define
        \[
            h_1=\prod_{\substack{q\in[\mu_1]\\ x_q\neq y_q}} (x_q\quad y_q)\qquad\in C_t. 
        \]
        We have
        $R_1(u)=\{x_1,\ldots,x_{\mu_1}\}=\{h_1(y_1),\ldots,h_1(y_{\mu_1})\}\subseteq
        R_1(h_1t)$. Since $C_{h_1t}=h_1C_th_1^{-1}=C_t$, we see that
        $\kappa_{h_1t=\kappa_t}$ and hence $\kappa_{h_1t}\{u\}\neq 0$. 

        Then let $R_2(u)=\{x_1',\ldots,x_{\mu_2}'\}$ and let $t'=h_1t$. Suppose
        $t'((i_q',j_q'))=x_q'$. If $i_q'\geq 2$ then let $y_q'=t'((2,j_q'))$
        Define $h_2$ similarly as above, i.e.\ 
        \[
            h_2=\prod_{\substack{q\in[\mu_2]\\ i_q'\geq 2 \\ x_q'\neq y_q'}}
            (x_q'\quad y_q')\qquad\in C_{t'}=C_t. 
        \]
        This is so that $R_2(u)=\{x_1',\ldots,x_{\mu_2}'\}=\{x_q'\;|\; i_q'\geq
        2\}\sqcup \{x_q'\;|\; i_q'=1\}=\{h_2(y_q')\}\sqcup \{x_q'\;|\;
        i_q'=1\}$, which lies in $R_2(h_2t')\sqcup R_1(h_1t')$. But
        $R_1(u)\subseteq R_1(t')=R_1(h_2t')$ and hence
        $\bigsqcup_{i=1}^\ell R_i(u)\subseteq \bigsqcup_{i=1}^\ell
        R_i(h_2h_1t)$ for $\ell=1,2$. 

        Continuing inductively, the statement~\ref{item:3.10.1} follows.

        Now we have 
        \[
            \sum_{i=1}^\ell \mu_i = \sum_{i=1}^\ell |R_i(u)|
            \qquad \underset{\ref{item:3.10.1}}{\leq}\qquad  \sum_{i=1}^\ell |R_i(ht)| =
            \sum_{i=1}^\ell \lambda_i 
        \]
        for all $\ell\in \{1,\ldots, \min(r,s)\}$, thereby
        proving~\ref{item:3.10.2}.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}
    For $\lambda,\mu\vdash n$, suppose $0\neq \varphi\in
    \Hom_{\sym{n}}(S^\lambda, M^\mu)$. If there exists a $\wt\varphi\in
    \Hom_{\sym{n}}(M^\lambda, M^\mu)$ extending $\varphi$, then $\lambda
    \trianglerighteq \mu$.

    \begin{proof}
        We know $S^\lambda$ is cyclic, generated by $e_t$ for any $t\in
        \Delta^\lambda$. Thus $\varphi(e_t)\neq 0$, since $\varphi$ is non-zero. Fix $t\in \Delta^\lambda$.
        If $\wt\varphi$ is as given, then \[0\neq \varphi(e_t)=\wt\varphi(e_t) =
        \wt\varphi(\kappa_t\{t\}) = \kappa_t\wt\varphi(\{t\}).\]
        Write $\varphi(\{t\})$ as a sum of $\mu$-tabloids to see that there
        exists a $u\in \Delta^\mu$ such that $\kappa_t\{u\}\neq 0$. We can then
        conclude by the previous proposition.
    \end{proof}
\end{theorem}

    \ker(f)$ then $\lambda\trianglerighteq \mu$. If $\lambda=\mu$, then $f$ is a
    scalar.

    $0\neq f(e_t)=f(\kappa_t(t))=\kappa_t(f(\{t\}))$. But $f(\{t\})$ is a linear
    combination of $\mu$-tabloids so $\lambda\triangleright\mu$ by the

    So $f(e_t)$ is a scalar multiple of $e_t$ for any $t$. Since $\sym{n}$ acts
    transitively on $e_t$s, it must be the same scalar for all $e_t$s.
\end{example}
