This is a fairly gentle introduction to the representation theory of the
symmetric group. The flavour will be both algebraic and combinatorial, in
particular a first course on representation theory is prerequisite.

Why study representations of symmetric groups? Firstly it is a nice theory. For
instance, there is a \emph{canonical} classification of the irreducibles ---
irreducible representations of $\sym{n}$ over $\bbC$ are parametrised by
\emph{partitions} of $n$. The construction works uniformly for all symmetric
groups, much unlike the case of general finite groups where multiple ad hoc
constructions are needed for each case. 

\emph{Introduction to diagram algebras}. Another reason is that the theory of
symmetric groups serves as fodder for the theory for other algebras that are
diagrammatic in flavour, such as (pure) braid groups.

\emph{Schur--Weyl duality}. Here is a third reason the representation theory
of the symmetric group is nice. For $V$ a $\bbC$-vector space, the tensor power
$V^{\otimes 2}$ is a representation of $\mathsf{GL}(V)\times \sym{2}$ where
$\mathsf{GL}(V)$ acts on each factor, and $\sym{2}$ acts by permuting factors
(i.e.\ $v\otimes w\mapsto w\otimes v$). Then $V^{\otimes 2}$ splits into
irreducible representations as $\Sym^2 V \oplus \Exter^2 V$; there exists a
correspondence between representations of $\sym{2}$ (namely the trivial and sign
representation) and those of $\mathsf{GL}(V)$ (the two factors of $V^{\otimes
2}$ respectively). \emph{Schur--Weyl duality} extends this theory from $\sym{2}$
to $\sym{r}$. That is, consider the representation $V^{\otimes r}$ of
$\GL{V}\times \sym{r}$ where $\GL{V}$ acts on each factor and $\sym{r}$ permutes
the factors. Then Schur--Weyl duality shows $V^{\otimes r}=\bigoplus_i
A_i\otimes B_i$ where $\{A_i\}$ are irreducible representations of $\GL{V}$ and
$\{B_i\}$ are irreducible representations of $\sym{r}$. Again, there is a
correspondence $A_i\leftrightarrow B_i$ between some representations of $\GL{V}$
and some representations of $\sym{r}$.

Alas this correspondence is not usually bijective --- one issue is that $\GL{V}$
has infinitely many irreducible representations while $\sym{r}$ has a finite
number. Another problem is taking inverse powers of determinants. 

But the correspondence is \emph{almost} bijective if we stabilise. If we let $\dim(V)$
be large then we recover all representations of $\sym{r}$, while if we let $r$
be large then we recover all representations of $\GL{V}$ up to powers of the
determinant. The details are beyond the scope of this course. 

\emph{Topology}. A fourth reason comes by taking any topological space $X$
and considering its \emph{configuration space} $\sf{C}^nX$ (i.e.\ the moduli of
$n$ distinct unordered points in $X$). The group $\sym{n}$ naturally acts on
$\sf{C}^n X$, giving a representation of $\sym{n}$ on $\HH^i(\sf{C}^nX, \bbC)$.

\emph{Behaviour in positive characteristic.} Representations over fields of
prime characteristic can be constructed in a `similar' manner to the
characteristic zero theory, which is again unlike the general case for finite
groups (modular representation theory tends to be much, much harder in
prime characteristic than over $\bbC$; even constructing characters is hard).

\section{Representations and modules}
\marginpar{Lecture 1, 12/10/26} 
We go over some standard results in the representation theory of a finite group
$G$ over a field $\bbk$. By \emph{module}, we will always mean a finite
dimensional left module.

\begin{definition} 
    A \emph{representation} of $G$ on a finite dimensional $\bbk$-vector space
    $V$ is any (and all) of the following.
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item a homomorphism $\rho\colon G\to \GL{V}$;
        \item a linear action of $G$ on $V$, i.e.\ a map $G\times V \to V$ such
            that for all $g,h\in G$ and $v,w\in V$ we have $(gh)(v)=g(h(v))$,
            $1(v)=v$, $g(\alpha v + \beta w)=\alpha(gv)+\beta(gw)$;
        \item a $\bbk G$-module structure on $V$, where $\bbk G$ is the
            \emph{group algebra} (the free $\bbk$-module with basis $G$, with
            product given by extending the multiplication in $G$ linearly). 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    The one-dimensional trivial representation of $G$, denoted $\bbOne_G$, is
    given by $V=\bbk$ with $g(v)=v$ for all $g\in G$, $v\in V$.
\end{example}

If $W$ is a subrepresentation of $V$ (i.e.\ a $\bbk G$-submodule) then we write
$W\leq V$. A representation $V$ is \emph{simple} (or \emph{irreducible}) if it
is a simple $\bbk G$-module, i.e.\ it is non-zero and its only
subrepresentations are $\mathbf{0}$ and $V$. We say $V$ is \emph{semisimple} (or
\emph{complete reducible}) if it is a direct sum of simple representations (this
property is closed under taking sub-objects and quotients). We say $V$ is
\emph{indecomposable} if it is not a direct sum of proper subrepresentations, we
say it is \emph{decomposable} otherwise. 

\begin{theorem}[(Maschke)]
    If $\bbk$ has characteristic zero, then every subrepresentation of $V$ is in
    fact a direct summand. In other words, irreducible and indecomposable
    representations of $V$ coincide.
\end{theorem}

A \emph{$G$-homomorphism} between two $\bbk G$-modules $V,W$ is a $\bbk G$-linear
map $\alpha\colon V\to W$.

\begin{theorem}[(Schur's lemma)]
    Suppose $V,W$ are irreducible representations of $G$. Any $G$-homomorphism
    $V\to W$ is either zero or is an isomorphism. In particular, $\End_GV$ is
    a division algebra over $\bbk$ (if $\bbk=\ol\bbk$ then $\End_GV\simeq \bbk$).
\end{theorem}

Suppose $\{L_i\}$ is a collection of pairwise non-isomorphic irreducible
representations of $G$, and $V$ is semisimple. Then there is an isomorphism
$f\colon \bigoplus L_i ^{\oplus n_i}\to V$ for some non-negative integers $n_i$. The
isomorphism is not canonical, but the embedding $f(L_i^{\oplus n_i})\into V$
\emph{is} --- the image $f(L_i^{\oplus n_i})$, called the \emph{$L_i$-isotypic
piece}, is the sum of the images of all maps $L_i\to V$. Thus each semisimple $\bbk
G$-module $V$ is \emph{canonically} isomorphic to the direct sum of its isotypic
components, and each $L_i$-isotypic component admits a non-canonical
decomposition into a direct sum of $L_i$s.

\marginpar{Lecture 2, 14/10/26} 
\begin{definition}
    For $G$-representations $L_i, V$ as above, the \emph{$L_i$-multiplicity space} in $V$ is $\Hom_G(L_i, V)=M_i$.
\end{definition}

Suppose $\bbk$ is algebraically closed, and $V$ is a finite dimensional
isotypic representation of $G$ (that is, $V\simeq L_i^{\oplus n_i}$ for some
irreducible representation $L_i$). In this case, the canonical map $\Hom_G(L_i,
V)\otimes L_i\to V$ is an isomorphism. If $V$ is semisimple and $\{M_i\}$ are the
multiplicity spaces of irreducible representations $\{L_i\}$, then the
analogously defined map $\bigoplus M_i\otimes L_i \to V$ is an isomorphism. 

If $V$ is not semisimple, then the map $\bigoplus \Hom_G(L_i,V)\otimes L_i\to V$
is only injective, and its image is called the \emph{socle} of $V$.

\begin{proposition}
    The left regular representation of $G$, given by $V=\bbk G$ where $G$ acts by left
    multiplication, is semisimple and has a $(\dim L_i)$-dimensional
    multiplicity space for each irreducible representation $L_i$. In other words
    it decomposes as \[V=\bigoplus_{L_i\in \textnormal{Irr}(G)} L_i^{\oplus \dim
    L_i}.\]
    It follows that $|G|=\sum (\dim L_i)^2$.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Maschke's theorem again.} Call a $\bbk G$-module $P$
\emph{projective} if every short exact sequence of $\bbk G$-modules $0\to N\to
N'\xrightarrow{\alpha} P\to 0$ splits, i.e.\ there exists a map $\beta\colon
P\to N'$ with $\alpha\beta = \id_P$. Every representation of $\bbk G$ is
semisimple if and only if the trivial representation $\bbOne_G$ is projective.
Then Maschke's theorem states that if $G$ is a finite group and the
characteristic of $\bbk$ does not divide $|G|$, then $\bbOne_G$ is projective
and hence every representation of $G$ is semisimple.

\begin{remark}
    If the characteristic of $\bbk$ divides $|G|$, then this \emph{always}
    fails. For example take $G=\bbZ/p$ and $\bbk = \bbF_p$, and consider the
    representation $V=\bbF_p^{\oplus 2}$ where $a\in G$ acts as
    $\mat{1&a\\0&1}$. Then $V$ is a nontrivial self-extension of the trivial
    representation $\bbOne_G$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Tensor products.} The \emph{tensor product} $V\otimes_\bbk
W$ of two $G$-representations $V,W$ has the structure of a $G$-module given by
$g(v\otimes w)=(gv) \otimes (gw)$. 

\paragraph{Induction and Restriction.} For $H\leq G$, there is a
\emph{restriction} functor $\text{Res}^G_H\colon \text{Rep}(G)\to \text{Rep}(H)$
where the $\bbk G$ module $V$ is viewed as a $\bbk H$-module in the obvious way.
We sometimes denote the restricted module as $\text{Res}^G_H V =
V\!\downarrow^G_H = V\!\downarrow_H$. It has a left adjoint, the \emph{induction
functor} $\text{Ind}^G_H\colon \text{Rep}(H)\to \text{Rep}(G)$ given by
$\text{Ind}^G_H(-)=\bbk G\otimes_{\bbk H }(-)$. We then have \emph{Frobenius
reciprocity}, \[\Hom_G(\text{Ind}^G_H U, V)= \Hom_H(U, \text{Res}^G_H V).\]

\paragraph{Permutation modules.} A \emph{permutation module} is a representation
of $G$ equipped with a basis on which $G$ acts, i.e.\ a basis $B$ such that
$gb\in B$ for all $g\in G$ and $b\in B$. In this case we denote the module
$\bbk[B]$. For example, the left regular
representation is naturally a permutation module.

\begin{proposition}
    If $G$ acts transitively on a finite set $X$, then the corresponding
    permutation module $\bbk [X]$ is isomorphic to the induced representation
    $\textnormal{Ind}_H^G(\bbOne_H)$ where $H\leq G$ is the stabiliser of any point
    $x\in X$, and $\bbOne_H$ is the trivial representation.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Duals.} If $V$ is a finite dimensional
$\bbk$-vector space, then so is $V\dual = \Hom_\bbk (V,\bbk)$. If $e_1,\ldots,
e_n$ is a basis of $V$ then $V\dual$ is naturally equipped with a dual basis
$\varepsilon_1,\ldots, \varepsilon_n$ such that
$\varepsilon_i(e_j)=\delta_{ij}$. If $V$ is a $G$-representation, then so is $V\dual$ via $g\varphi = \varphi \circ g^{-1}$.

\paragraph{Some linear algebra.}
Suppose $V$ is equipped with a symmetric bilinear form $\langle \cdot, \cdot
\rangle$. This induces a natural map $V\to V\dual$ given by $v\mapsto \langle
\cdot, v\rangle$. Even if $\langle \cdot,\cdot \rangle$ is non-singular (i.e.\
$V\orth = 0$)  we could still have a subspace $W\subset V$ with $W\orth \cap W
\neq 0 $. We will be interested in how big such intersections can get. 

Let $e_1,\ldots, e_n$ be a basis of the subspace $W$. The \emph{Gram matrix} of
$W$ with respect to this basis is the $n\times n$ matrix $A$ with $(i,j)$th
entry $\langle e_i, e_j\rangle$.

\begin{proposition}
    The dimension of $W/(W\cap W\orth)$ is the rank of the Gram matrix.

    \begin{proof}
        The induced map $W\to W\dual$ given by $w\mapsto \langle \cdot,
        w\rangle$ is precisely the map given by the Gram matrix when we equip
        $W, W\dual$ with dual bases. The kernel of the map is $W\cap W\orth$, so
        the answer follows from rank--nullity.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Character theory.} Work over the field $\bbk = \bbC$, and let $V$ be
a finite dimensional $\bbC G$-module. The \emph{character} of $V$ is a function
$\chi_V\colon G\to \bbC$ given by $g\mapsto \text{tr}(g\colon V\to
V)$. This is a \emph{class function}, i.e.\ it is invariant under conjugation.
Some useful calculations --- 
\begin{gather*}
    \chi_{(V\oplus W)} = \chi_V + \chi_W, \qquad \chi_{(V\dual)} = \ol{\chi_V},
    \qquad \chi_{(V\otimes W)} = \chi_V \cdot \chi_W, \qquad \chi_{\Hom(V,W)} =
    \ol{\chi_V}\cdot \chi_W, \\ 
    \text{for the permutation module determined by }G\circlearrowright B, \qquad
    \chi_{\bbk[B]}(g) =\#\{b\in B\;|\; gb = b\}.
\end{gather*}

Character theory is most useful because of the following result. 

\begin{proposition}
    The space of class functions on $G$ has basis given by the characters of
    irreducible representations.
\end{proposition}

In positive characteristic we need to use \emph{Brauer characters}, which we
won't dive into.

\section{Representations of the symmetric groups}
\marginpar{Lecture 3, 19/10/26} 
We now overview the combinatorial approach to representation theory of the
symmetric groups, via a certain \emph{diagram calculus}. Thus for a finite set
$X$, write $\Sym(X)=\{\text{bijections }X\to X\}$ for the \emph{symmetric group
of $X$}. In particular, the standard symmetric groups $\sym{n}$ are the same as
$\Sym([n])$ where $[n]$ denotes the set $\{1,\ldots, n\}$. Note also that
$\sym{0}$ is the trivial group.

While we are establishing conventions, also note that composition goes from
right to left: $(1\,2\,3)(1\,2)=(1\,3)$.

\paragraph{Representations we have already met.}
The group $\sym{n}$, like any other group, has the trivial representation which
we denote $\bbOne_{\sym{n}}$. There is only one other one-dimensional
representation, the \emph{alternating} or \emph{sign} representation, which we
denote $\sgn\colon \sym{n}\to \{\pm 1\}$.

There is also the natural representation $\sym{n}\circlearrowright \bbC^n$ given
by permuting the standard basis $\{e_1,\ldots, e_n\}$, called the \emph{natural} or the
\emph{permutation representation}. We write $V_n$ for this representation. Note
that the element $(e_1+\cdots + e_n)\in V_n$ is $\sym{n}$-invariant, so $V_n$ is
never irreducible (unless $n=1$).

We make the following observations about the natural representations $V_n$: 
\begin{gather*}
    V_n = (\bbOne_{\sym{n-1}})\uparrow^{\sym{n}}_{\sym{n-1}}\,, \qquad V_n
    \downarrow^{\sym{n}}_{\sym{n-1}} = V_{n-1} \oplus \bbOne_{\sym{n-1}}.
\end{gather*}

\begin{exercise}
    Define the \emph{augmentation map} $\varepsilon\colon V_n\to \bbC$ by
    $e_1\mapsto 1$. Show that if $n>1$, then $\ker(\varepsilon)$ is an irreducible
    $(n-1)$-dimensional $\sym{n}$-subrepresentation of $V_n$. We call this the
    \emph{standard representation of $\sym{n}$}.
\end{exercise}

\paragraph{Partitions.} Now recall that every element $\sigma\in \sym{n}$ can be
decomposed as a product $\sigma = \sigma_1\cdots \sigma_k$ of disjoint cycles
(in a way that every $i\in [n]$ appears in exactly one cycle).

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{partition $\lambda$ of $n$}, written $\lambda \vdash n$, is a
    non-increasing sequence of positive integers summing to $n$. That is,
    $\lambda$ is given by a tuple \(\lambda = (\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)\) of
    integers \(\lambda_1\geq \cdots \geq \lambda_r> 0\), satisfying
    \(\sum_{i=1}^r\lambda_i = n\). In this case we say the $\lambda_i$s are the \emph{parts} of $\lambda$, $n=|\lambda|$ is
    the \emph{size} of $\lambda$, and $r=\ell(\lambda)$ is the \emph{length} of
    $\lambda$. 

    Write $\calP(n)=\{\lambda\;|\; \lambda\vdash n\}$ for the set of all
    partitions of $n$.
\end{definition}

We adopt the convention that the length-zero tuple $\varnothing = ()$ is a partition
of $0$, called the \emph{empty partition}. We also adopt a condensed notation
that collapses equal parts together, for example we write $(4,3^2,1)$ for the
partition $(4,3,3,1)\vdash 11$.

Note that partitions of $n$ are naturally in bijection with cycle types, and
thus conjugacy classes in $\sym{n}$. For example, the three partitions $(3)$,
$(2,1)$, and $(1^3)$ corresponds to the three classes (3-cycles, transpositions,
and identity) in $\sym{3}$.

\begin{definition} The \emph{Young diagram} of a partition
    $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ is 
$[\lambda] = \{(i,j)\in \bbN^2\;|\; 1\leq i\leq r, \quad 1\leq j \leq
\lambda_i\}$.
\end{definition}

We draw Young diagrams with boxes (instead of lattice points), with rows and
columns numbered as in a matrix. For example, here are all the partitions of
$4$ and their Young diagrams.
\[
    \ytableausetup{boxsize=1em}
    {
    \setlength{\arraycolsep}{2em}
    \begin{array}{ccccc}
        (4) & (3,1) & (2^2) & (2,1^2) & (1^4)\\
        \ydiagram{4} & \ydiagram{3,1} & \ydiagram{2,2} &\ydiagram{2,1,1}
                     &\ydiagram{1,1,1,1}
    \end{array}
    }
\]

\begin{definition} 
    Given $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)\vdash n$, the
    \emph{conjugate partition} $\lambda'$ is given by $\lambda'=(\mu_1,\ldots,
    \mu_{\lambda_1})$, where $\mu_j = \{i\in [r]\;|\; \lambda_i \geq j\}\). That
    is, $\lambda'$ is the partition whose Young diagram contains $(i,j)$ if and
    only if $(j,i)\in [\lambda]$.
\end{definition}

Thus pictorially, conjugation corresponds to flipping Young diagrams along the
diagonal. For example, $(4,3,1)$ and
$(3,2^2,1)$ are conjugate partitions of $8$. 
\[
    \ydiagram{4,3,1} \qquad \ydiagram{3,2,2,1}
    \ytableausetup{boxsize=normal}
\]


\begin{definition}
    Let $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ and $\mu=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_s)$ be
    partitions of $n\in \bbN$. We say $\lambda$ \emph{dominates} $\mu$, written
    $\lambda \unrhd \mu$, if $\sum_1^j\lambda_i\geq \sum_1^j\mu_i$ for all
    $j\in\{1,\ldots,\min(r,s)\}.$
\end{definition}

This is a partial order on $\calP(n)$, for example $(4,3,1)$ and
$(5,1^3)$ are incomparable in $\calP(8)$. Below is the Hasse diagram for
$\calP(6)$.
\[
    \begin{tikzcd}[column sep=small]
        &&& (4,1^2)\arrow[dr] && (3,1^3)\arrow[dr] &&& \\
        (6)\rar & (5,1)\rar & (4,2)\arrow[ur]\arrow[dr] && (3,2,1)\arrow[ur]\arrow[dr] && (2^2,1^2)\rar & (2,1^4)\rar & (1^6)\\
        &&& (3,3)\arrow[ur] && (2^3)\arrow[ur] &&&
    \end{tikzcd}
\]

There is also the \emph{lexicographic
order} on $\calP(n)$, where for distinct partitions
$\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ and $\mu=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_r)$ of $n$ we
say $\lambda>\mu$ if and only if there exists an $i\in \{1,\ldots, \min(r,s)\}$
such that $\lambda_i>\mu_i$, and $\lambda_j = \mu_j$ for $j=1,\ldots, i-1$. It
is easy to see that the lexicographic order \emph{extends} the domination order,
i.e.\ if $\lambda\unrhd \mu$ then $\lambda \geq \mu$. The converse is false,
since the lexicographic order is always a total order --- for example, the order
on $\calP(6)$ is given by
\[(6)\;>\;(5,1)\;>\;(4,2)\;>\;(4,1^2)\;>\;(3^2)\;>\;(3,2,1)\;>\;(3,1^3)\;>\;(2^3)\;>\;(2^2,1^2)\;>\;(2,1^4)\;>\;(1^6).\]

\begin{definition} 
    Given a partition $\lambda\vdash n$, a \emph{$\lambda$-tableau} (or a
    \emph{Young tableau of shape $\lambda$}) is a bijection $[\lambda]\to [n]$.
    We write $\Delta^\lambda$ for the set of all $\lambda$-tableaux.
\end{definition}

A $\lambda$-tableau can be naturally denoted by `filling in' the Young diagram
with numbers $1,\ldots, n$ such that each number appears once. For example, here

The action of $\sym{n}$ on $[n]$ extends to a permutation action on
$\Delta^\lambda$, namely $\sigma\in \sym{n}$ sends a bijection $t\in
\Delta^\lambda$ to $\sigma\circ t$. Pictorially this applies $\sigma$ to the
entry in each box, as shown below.
\[
    (1\,2\,3)\cdot \tableau{234,1} = \tableau{314,2}
\]

\begin{definition}
    Given a partition $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ and $t\in
    \Delta^\lambda$, for each $1\leq i \leq r$ (resp.\ $1\leq j\leq\lambda_1$) we write
    \[
        \mathsf{R}_i(t)=\{t(i,j)\;|\; 1\leq j\leq \lambda_i\} \qquad
        \left(\text{resp.}\quad \mathsf{C}_j(t)=\{t(i,j)\;|\; 1\leq i\leq
        (\lambda')_j\}\;\right)
    \]
    for the set of entries in the $i$th row (resp.\ $j$th column) of the tableau $t$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    For $\lambda\vdash n$, we say two $\lambda$-tableau $t,s\in \Delta^\lambda$
    are \emph{row-equivalent} (written $t\sim_R s$) if
    $\mathsf{R}_i(t)=\mathsf{R}_i(s)$ for all $i$. This is an equivalence
    relation on $\Delta^\lambda$, and its equivalence classes are called
    \emph{$\lambda$-tabloids}. We write $\{t\}$ for the tabloid corresponding to
    $t\in \Delta^\lambda$, and denote the set of $\lambda$-tabloids by
    $\Omega^\lambda=\Delta^\lambda/\sim_R$. 
\end{definition}

Pictorially we will denote these using horizontal lines as below.
\[
    \left\{\tableau{123,4}\right\}=\tabloid{123,4}=\tabloid{213,4}=\tabloid{321,4}
    \qquad \left(\neq \tabloid{412,3} \right).
\]

\paragraph{Representations from tabloids.} Given $\lambda\vdash n$ there is a natural action of $\sym{n}$ on
$\Omega^\lambda$, induced by that on $\Delta^\lambda$. Both produce permutation
representations; it is easy to see that $\bbC [\Delta^\lambda]$ is the regular
representation $\bbC\sym{n}$. We denote the permutation representation on
$\Omega^\lambda$ by \[M^\lambda = \bbC[\Omega^\lambda].\]

What is this representation?

\begin{lemma}
    Take $\lambda = (\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)\vdash n$. Then
    $M_\lambda=(\bbOne_{\sym{\lambda}})\uparrow^{\sym{n}}_{\sym{\lambda}}$, where
    $\sym{\lambda}= \sym{\lambda_1}\times \cdots \times \sym{\lambda_r}$. 
    \begin{proof}
        The action of $\sym{n}$ on $\Delta^\lambda$ (and hence $\Omega^\lambda$) is evidently transitive, and
        for any $t\in \Delta^\lambda$, the stabiliser of $\{t\}$ is the subgroup
        $\text{Sym}(\mathsf{R}_1(t))\times \cdots \times
        \text{Sym}(\mathsf{R}_r(t))\simeq \sym{\lambda}$.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

We will show that, over $\bbk = \bbC$, all but one irreducible summand of
$M^\lambda$ appear in $M^\mu$ for some $\mu \rhd
\lambda$; thus each $M^\lambda$ determines a unique irreducible representation
$L^\lambda$.

\begin{remark}
    We say any subgroup of $\sym{n}$ isomorphic to $\sym{\lambda}$ is a 
    \emph{Young subgroup} of type $\lambda$. Note all Young subgroups of a given
    type are conjugate to each other in $\sym n$.

    A $\lambda$-partition of $[n]$, i.e.\ a set-partition of type
    $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$, is
    a decomposition $n=\bigsqcup_{1}^r A_i$ with each $\# A_i=\lambda_i$. 
    The set of $\lambda$-partitions has a natural $\sym{n}$ action. There is a Young subgroup of type $\lambda$ for each such set partition; if
    \[P=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^r\left\{\sum_{j=1}^i\lambda_j + 1,\;\ldots\;, \sum_{j=1}^i
    \lambda_j + \lambda_{i+1}\right\}\]  then the stabiliser
    of $P$ is a Young subgroup of type $\lambda$. Hence
    $\bbC[\sym{n}/\sym{\lambda}]=\bbC[\text{set of }\lambda\text{-partitions}]$.
    This is sometimes called the \emph{generalised orbit-stabiliser theorem};
    this perspective becomes fundamental when working with other diagram
    algebras that aren't the symmetric group.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
    Take $n=9$, $\lambda=(4,3,2)$. Then there are
    $\binom{9}{4}\binom{5}{3}\binom{2}{2}=1260$ Young subgroups of type
    $\lambda$ in $\sym n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    The sets of tabloids for small partitions can be computed explicitly, for
    example we have
    \[
        \Omega^{(n)}=\left\{\tabloid{1\ldots n}\right\}, \qquad
        \Omega^{(n-1,1)}=\left\{\tabloid{1\ldots\quad {$i-$1}\quad{$i+$1}\quad \ldots n,
        {$i$}}\;\middle\vert\; 1\leq i\leq n\right\}.
    \]
    Thus $\sym {(n)}= \sym n$ and $\sym{(n-1,1)}=\sym {n-1}\times \sym{1}\simeq
    \sym{n-1}$. It follows that $M^{(n)}=\bbOne_{\sym n}$ is trivial, while
    $M^{(n-1,1)}=(\bbOne_{\sym {n-1}})\uparrow^{\sym n}_{\sym {n-1}}$ is the
    natural permutation representation $V_n$. 

    Likewise, noting that
    $\Omega^{(1,\ldots,1)}=\Delta^{(1,\ldots,1)}$, we see that
    $M^{(1,\ldots,1)}$ is the regular representation.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Representations from polytabloids, or Specht modules.} We now
introduce a construction central to the representation theory of symmetric
groups; these will be certain submodules of the representations
$M^\lambda$ we have already met. The goal is to (eventually) index simple $\bbC \sym n$-modules by partitions $\lambda\vdash n$, by showing
that for any simple $S$ the set $\{\mu\vdash n \;|\; S \subseteq M^\mu\}$ has a
unique minimal element $\lambda$ in the dominance order, and that this
correspondence is bijective so that the simple module can be denoted
$S^\lambda$. This dream will be realised in the next section, but an explicit
calculation is instructive.

\begin{example}
    Recall that $M^{(4)}$ is the trivial representation $\text{triv}$, hence so
    is $S^{(4)}$. On the other hand $M^{(3,1)}$ is the natural representation
    $V_4=\text{triv}\oplus\text{std}$, so we must have that
    $S^{(3,1)}=\text{std}$ is the standard representation. The representation $M^{(2,2)}$ decomposes as $\text{triv}\oplus \text{std}\oplus V$, so
    that $S^{(2,2)}=V$. Likewise, $M^{(2,1,1)}=\text{triv}\oplus
    \text{std}^{\oplus 2} \oplus V \oplus (\text{std}\otimes \text{sgn})$ whence
    the last factor is $S^{(2,1,1)}$. Lastly, recall that $M^{(1,1,1,1)}$ is
    simply the group algebra $\bbC \sym{4}$ itself so that $S^{(1,1,1,1)}$ is
    the only irreducible representation we haven't encountered yet (namely,
    $\text{sgn}$).
\end{example}

The submodules $S^\lambda\subseteq M^\lambda$ will in fact be described explicitly
as spans of \emph{polytabloids}.

\begin{definition}
    Given $\lambda\vdash n$, the \emph{row stabiliser} of $t\in \Delta^\lambda$
    is $R_t=\{\sigma\in \sym n\;|\; \sigma \cdot
    \mathsf{R}_i(t)=\mathsf{R}_i(t)\text{ for all }i\}$. The \emph{column stabiliser} 
    $C_t$ is defined likewise as the subgroup of $\sym n$ fixing each $\mathsf{C}_i(t)$.
    The \emph{column symmetriser} of $t$ is the element of the group algebra \[\kappa_t = \sum_{\sigma\in
    C_t}\text{sgn}(\sigma)\cdot \sigma \qquad \in \bbk \sym n.\] 
    The \emph{polytabloid} corresponding to $t$ is the element
    \[e_t=\kappa_t\{t\}\qquad \in M^\lambda.\] We will sometimes denote it by $e(t)$,
    noting dependence on the tableau $t$ (and not just the tabloid
    $\{t\}$).

\end{definition}


\begin{example} For $n=5$ and $t=\smalltableau{241,35}\in \Delta^{(3,2)}$, the above correspond to 
    \begin{gather*}
        R_t = \langle (2\,4),(4\,1), (3\,5)\rangle \subseteq \sym 5, \qquad C_t=\langle (2\,3),(4\,5)\rangle \subseteq
        \sym 5, \\[0.7em]
        \kappa_t = 1-(2\,3)-(4\,5)+(2\,3)(4\,5), \\[0.7em]
        e_t =
        \tabloid{241,35}-\tabloid{341,25}-\tabloid{251,34}+\tabloid{351,24}.
    \end{gather*}
\end{example}
\medskip
\marginpar{Lecture 5, 26/10/26} 

\begin{definition}
    Given $\lambda\vdash n$, the \emph{Specht module} $S^\lambda$ is
    the submodule \(\langle e_t\;|\; t\in \Delta^\lambda \rangle \subset
    M^\lambda\) spanned by polytabloids corresponding to tableaux of shape
    $\lambda$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:polytabloidaction}
    For $\lambda \vdash n$ and $t\in \Delta^\lambda$, we have
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item\label{item:specht1} $e_t\neq 0$, 
        \item\label{item:specht2} for all $\sigma\in\sym{n}$, $\sigma\cdot e_t =
            e_{\sigma t}$, and 
        \item\label{item:specht3} for all $\sigma\in C_t$, $\sigma\cdot e_t =
            \textnormal{sgn}(\sigma)\cdot e_t$. 
    \end{enumerate}
    In particular, $S^\lambda$ is a \emph{cyclic} submodule of $M^\lambda$,
    i.e.\ $S^\lambda=\bbk \sym{n}\cdot e_t$ for some (equivalently any) $t\in
    \Delta^\lambda$.

    \begin{proof}
        To prove~\ref{item:specht1}, note that $R_t\cap C_t=\{1\}$ and hence if
        $\sigma\in C_t$, then $\sigma\{t\} = \{t\}$ implies $\sigma=1$. The
        coefficient of the tabloid $\{t\}$ in $e_t$ is
        $\textnormal{sgn}(1)=1\neq 0$, so $e_t$ is non-zero.
        
        Now it is clear that $C_{\sigma t} = \sigma C_t \sigma^{-1}$,
        so~\ref{item:specht2} follows from the calculation below ---
        \begin{align*}
            \sigma \cdot e_t 
            = \sum_{\tau\in C_t} \textnormal{sgn}(\tau)\cdot \sigma \tau\{t\} 
            = \sum_{\tau \in C_t}\textnormal{sgn}(\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1})\cdot
            (\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1})\{\sigma t\} 
            = \sum_{\omega\in C_{\sigma
            t}}\textnormal{sgn}(\omega)\cdot \omega\{\sigma t\} = e_{\sigma t}.
        \end{align*}
        To prove~\ref{item:specht3}, note that if $\sigma\in C_t$ then we have
        $\tau\in C_t\iff \sigma\tau\in C_t$, whence
        \[
            \sigma\cdot e_t = \sum_{\tau\in C_t}
            \textnormal{sgn}(\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1})\cdot \sigma\tau\{t\} =
            \sum_{\sigma\tau\in C_t} \textnormal{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\cdot
            \textnormal{sgn}(\sigma)\cdot \sigma\tau\{t\} =
            \textnormal{sgn}(\sigma)\cdot  e_t.
        \]
        Lastly, to show that $S^\lambda$ is cyclic we note
        that $\sym{n}$ acts transitively on $\Delta^\lambda$, so
        that~\ref{item:specht2} allows the conclusion.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{exercise}
    Use the fact that $R_t\cap C_t= \{1\}$ to show that $e_t$ is a signed sum of
    $|C_t|$ distinct $\lambda$-tabloids.
\end{exercise}

\begin{example}
    If $\lambda=(n)$, note that $M^{(n)}$ is the trivial module $\bbOne_{\sym{n}}$
    and hence the non-zero submodule $S^{(n)}\subseteq M^{(n)}$ must also be
    $\bbOne_{\sym{n}}$. On the other hand if $\lambda=(1^n)$ then $C_t=\sym{n}$,
    so the above lemma shows $\sigma e_t = \textnormal{sgn}(\sigma)\cdot e_t$ for all
    $\sigma\in \sym{n}$ and all $t\in \Delta^\lambda$. Thus $\dim (S^{(1^n)})=1$,
    and $S^{(1^\lambda)} = \bbk \sym{n}\cdot e_t$ is the sign representation of
    $\sym{n}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    Consider the partition $(2,1)\vdash 3$, defining the Specht module
    $S^{(2,1)}$. This module has dimension $2$; to see this note that
    $S^{(2,1)}$ is spanned by the polytabloids    
    \[
        e\left(\tableau{31,2}\right)=
        \tabloid{31,2}-\tabloid{21,3}, \qquad 
        e\left(\tableau{12,3}\right)=\tabloid{12,3}-\tabloid{32,1},\qquad
        e\left(\tableau{23,1}\right)=\tabloid{23,1}-\tabloid{13,2},
    \] 
    where we observe that other tabloids can be realised as
    $e(\smalltableau{32,1})=-e(\smalltableau{12,3})$ and such. Note the above
    polytabloids sum to zero, but any two of them are linearly independent. Thus
    $S^{(2,1)}$ is two-dimensional. 
\end{example}

\section{Classifying irreducible representations}

We now prove that the Specht modules
$\{S^\lambda\;|\;\lambda\vdash n\}$ form a complete set of irreducible
$\bbC\sym{n}$-modules. 

\paragraph{James' submodule theorem.} Note that (over any field $\bbk$) the
vector space underlying $M^\lambda$ is canonically equipped with a basis given
by tabloids. The standard inner product $\langle \cdot,\cdot \rangle \colon
M^\lambda\times M^\lambda\to \bbC$ with respect to this basis, given by linearly
extending the assignment $\langle \{t\},\{t'\}\rangle=\delta_{\{t\}\{t'\}}$ for
$t,t'\in \Delta^\lambda$, is evidently $\sym n$-invariant.

The \emph{orthogonal complement} $N\orth$ of a subspace $N\subset M^\lambda$ is
denoted $N\orth = \{m\in M^\lambda\;|\; \langle m,n\rangle = 0 \text{ for all }n\in N\}$. Note that if $N$ is a $\sym{n}$-submodule of $M^\lambda$ then so is $N\orth$,
by $\sym n$-invariance of the inner product. 

\begin{theorem}[(James' submodule theorem)]
    Given $\lambda \vdash n$ and a $\sym n$-subrepresentation $V\subseteq M^\lambda$,
    we have either $S^\lambda\subseteq V$ or $V\subseteq (S^{\lambda})\orth$ (or
    both).
\end{theorem}

James' theorem will follow from the following calculation.

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:technical1}
    \marginpar{Lecture 6, 28/10/26} 
    Let $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots, \lambda_r)$ be a partition of $n$, and
    $t,u\in \Delta^\lambda$ such that $\kappa_t\{u\}\neq 0$. Then there is an
    $h\in C_t$ such that $h\{t\}=\{u\}$.

    \begin{proof}
        We must construct $h\in C_t$ such that
        $\mathsf{R}_i(ht)=\mathsf{R}_i(u)$ for all $i$. 

        Claim that if $\kappa_t\{u\}\neq 0$, then any
        numbers $x\neq y$ appearing in the same row of $u$ must appear in
        different columns of $t$. If not, then the transposition $(x\,y)$ lies
        in $C_t$. Let $\mathcal{T}$ be a set of (left) coset representatives of
        $\langle (x\,y)\rangle$ in $C_t$, so that $C_t=\mathcal{T}\cdot \id
        \;\cup\; \mathcal{T}\cdot (x\,y)$. Then 
        \[\kappa_t = \sum_{\sigma\in C_t}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot\sigma = \sum_{\tau\in
        \mathcal{T}}\sgn(\tau)\cdot \tau(1-(x\,y))\]
        and hence
        \[\kappa_t\{u\}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\sgn(\tau)\cdot
        \tau(\{u\}-(x\,y)\{u\})\]
        which vanishes since $(x\,y)\in R_u$ (since $x,y$ lie in the same row in
        $u$). This proves the claim.

        Now suppose $\mathsf{R}_1(u)=\{x_1,\ldots,x_{\lambda_1}\}$ and that $x_s$ belongs
        to column $j_s$ of $t$, for each $s\in [\lambda_1]$. The $j_s$s are
        pairwise distinct by the above claim. Let $y_s=t((1,j_s))$, and define
        \[h_1=(x_1\,y_1)(x_2\,y_2)\cdots(x_{\lambda_1}\,y_{\lambda_1}) \qquad
        \in C_t\] 
        where $(x_i\,y_i)=\text{id}$ if $x_i=y_i$.
        Note $\mathsf{R}_1(h_1 t)=\{h_1y_1, \ldots, h_1y_{\lambda_1}\} =
        \{x_1,\ldots,x_{\lambda_1}\}=\mathsf{R}_1(u)$. Since $h_1\in C_t$, we see that
        $C_{h_1t}=h_1C_th_1^{-1}=C_t$, and hence $\kappa_t=\kappa_{h_1t}$ does
        not vanish on $\{u\}$.

        Continuing, suppose $\mathsf{R}_2(u)=\{x_1',\ldots, x_{\lambda_2}'\}$
        with $x_s'$ belonging to column $j_s'$ of $t'=h_1t$. By the claim, the
        $j_s'$s are pairwise
        distinct. Again, let $y_s'=t((2,j_s'))$ and define
        \[h_2=(x_1'\,y_1')(x_2'\,y_2')\cdots(x_{\lambda_2}'\,y_{\lambda_2}') \qquad\in C_{t'}=C_t.\]
        Again, $\mathsf{R}_2(h_2\,t')=\mathsf{R}_2(u)$ and
        $\mathsf{R}_1(h_2t')=\mathsf{R}_1(t')=\mathsf{R}_1(u)$. In conclusion,
        $\mathsf{R}_i(h_2h_1t)=\mathsf{R}_i(u)$ for
        both $i=1,2$. Iterating, we construct for each $m\in \{3,4,\ldots, r\}$
        an element $h_m\in C_t$ such that $\mathsf{R}_i(h_mh_{m-1}\ldots h_1
        t)=\mathsf{R}_i(u)$ for all $i\in [m]$. Declaring $h=h_r\cdots h_1$, we
        have $h\{t\}=\{u\}$ as required.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:kappaaction}
    For any $\lambda\vdash n$ and tabloids $t,u\in \Delta^\lambda$, we have
    $\kappa_t\{u\}\in \{0,\pm e_t\}$. In particular, the image of $\kappa_t\colon
    M^\lambda\to M^\lambda$ is $\bbk e_t$.

    \begin{proof}
        If $\kappa_t\{u\}\neq 0$, then the above lemma shows there is a $h\in
        C_t$ with $h\{t\}=u$, and hence
        \[\kappa_t\{u\}=\kappa_t \{ht\}=\sgn(h)\cdot \kappa_t\{t\} =
        \sgn(h)\cdot e_t,\]
        which is $\pm e_t$ as required. It follows that the image of $\kappa_t$
        is contained in $\bbk e_t$, since tabloids span $M^\lambda$.
        Since $\kappa_t\{t\}=e_t$ by definition, the image is equal to $\bbk
        e_t$.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of James' submodule theorem]
    Let $\lambda\vdash n$ and $V\leq M^\lambda$ be as given, and assume
    $V\not\leq (S^\lambda)\orth$. Then there is a $v\in V$ and $t\in
    \Delta^\lambda$ such that $\langle v, e^t\rangle\neq 0$. Thus $\langle v,
    e_t\rangle = \sum_{\sigma\in C_t} \sgn (\sigma)\cdot \langle \sigma^{-1}v,
    \{t\}\rangle = \langle \kappa_t v, \{t\}\rangle$ is non-zero.

    In particular, $\kappa_t v\neq 0$, so $\kappa_t v = \beta e_t$ for some
    $\beta\in \bbk^\times$. Since $\kappa_t v\in V$, it follows that $e_t\in V$
    and thus $S^\lambda = \bbk \sym{n} \cdot e_t \leq V$ as required.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Irreducibility of Specht modules.} James' theorem
immediately gives a construction of irreducible $\bbk\sym n$-modules from the
Specht modules, over any field $\bbk$.

\begin{corollary}
    If $\lambda \vdash n$ then $S^\lambda/(S^\lambda \cap (S^\lambda) \orth)$ is
    either zero or irreducible.

    \begin{proof}
        If $S^\lambda\leq (S^\lambda)\orth$ then the quotient is zero.
        Otherwise, take any submodule $V\leq S^\lambda$. By James' submodule theorem,
        $V=S^\lambda$ or $V\leq S^\lambda \cap (S^\lambda)\orth$. That is,
        $S^\lambda\cap (S^\lambda)\orth$ is a maximal submodule of $S^\lambda$,
        so the quotient is irreducible as required.
    \end{proof}
\end{corollary}

Recall that a representation $\rho\colon G\to \mathsf{GL}_n(\bbk)$ is \emph{absolutely irreducible} if, for any field extension $\bbk\subseteq \bbK$, the corresponding representation $\ol\rho\colon G\to \mathsf{GL}_n(\bbK)$ is irreducible.

\begin{example} 
    Consider the cyclic group $C_4=\langle \alpha \rangle$, which has a two-dimensional
    $\bbQ$-representation given by $\alpha \mapsto \mat{0&1 \\ -1 &
    0}$. This representation is irreducible, but not absolutely irreducible ---
    indeed, upon extension to $\bbQ(\sqrt{-1})$, the generator $\ol\rho(\alpha)$
    has eigenvalues $\pm \sqrt{-1}$ and hence the representation splits.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
    \marginpar{Lecture 7, 2/11/26} 
    If $\lambda \vdash n$ then $S^\lambda/(S^\lambda \cap (S^\lambda) \orth)$ is
    either zero or \emph{absolutely} irreducible.

    \begin{proof}
        Choose a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ of polytabloids in $S^\lambda$. Note that
        $\dim(S^\lambda/S^\lambda\cap (S^\lambda)\orth)$ is the rank of the Gram
        matrix $A$ taken with respect to this basis. But the entries
        $A_{ij}=\langle e_i, e_j\rangle$ all lie in the prime subfield of $\bbk$
        (i.e.\ $\bbF_p$ or $\bbQ$) so the rank doesn't change upon
        extending $\bbk$. Since we have already shown $S^\lambda/(S^\lambda\cap
        (S^\lambda)\orth)$ is zero or irreducible over these fields, the result follows.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}
    If $\textnormal{char}(\bbk)=0$ then $S^\lambda$ is irreducible for all
    $\lambda$.

    \begin{proof}
        It suffices to show that $S^\lambda$ is absolutely irreducible over
        $\bbQ$, since any field of characteristic zero extends the field of
        rationals. But over $\bbQ$ the bilinear form $\langle \cdot, \cdot
        \rangle$ on $M^\lambda$ is positive definite, so $S^\lambda\cap
        (S^\lambda)\orth = 0$. The result follows.
    \end{proof}
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
    Over a general field where $S^\lambda$ may not be irreducible, it is still
    true that $M^\lambda$ has a unique indecomposable summand $Y^\lambda$ which
    contains $S^\lambda$. This summand is called the \emph{Young module} for
    $\lambda$. These correspond to projective modules for the general linear
    group.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Partitions and Specht modules.} We now turn to showing $\{S^\lambda\;|\; \lambda\vdash n\}$ is a
complete set of irreducible representations over a field of characteristic zero,
by first showing that the assignment $\lambda\mapsto S^\lambda$ is bijective.
For this, we begin by group-theoretically characterising the dominance order on partitions.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:dominancecharacterisation}
    Suppose $\lambda,\mu\vdash n$ are such that there exist tabloids $t\in
    \Delta^\lambda$, $u\in \Delta^\mu$ with $\kappa_t\{u\}\neq 0$. Then
    $\lambda \unrhd \mu$ in the dominance order.
\end{proposition}

We will in fact prove the following strengthening of this, which is akin
to~\cref{lem:technical1}. 

\begin{lemma}
    Suppose $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ and
    $\mu=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_s)$ are partitions of $n$. Let $t\in
    \Delta^\lambda$, $u\in \Delta^\mu$ be such that $\kappa_t\{u\}\neq
    0$. Then      there is an $h\in C_t$ such that for all $\ell\in
    \{1,2,\ldots,\min(r,s)\}$ we have \[\bigsqcup_{i=1}^\ell
    \mathsf{R}_i(u)\subseteq \bigsqcup_{i=1}^\ell \mathsf{R}_i(ht).\]

    \begin{proof}
        Claim that if distinct entries $x,y$ appear in the same row of $u$ then
        they appear in different columns of $t$; this is proved similarly as
        in~\cref{lem:technical1}. 

        Given this, suppose $\mathsf{R}_1(u)=\{x_1,\ldots, x_r\}$ and that $x_q$
        lies in column $j_q$ of $t$. By the claim, $j_q$s are pairwise distinct.
        Let $y_q=t((1,j_q))$ and define
        \[
            h_1= (x_1\,y_1)(x_2\,y_2)\cdots (x_{\mu_1}\,y_{\mu_1})\qquad\in C_t
        \]
        where $(x_i\,y_i)=\text{id}$ if $x_i=y_i$.
        We have
        \[\mathsf{R}_1(u)=\{x_1,\ldots,x_{\mu_1}\}=\{h_1y_1,\ldots,h_1y_{\mu_1}\}\subseteq
        \mathsf{R}_1(h_1t).\] Since $C_{h_1t}=h_1C_th_1^{-1}=C_t$, we see that
        $\kappa_{h_1t}=\kappa_t$ and hence $\kappa_{h_1t}\{u\}\neq 0$. 

        Continuing, let $\mathsf{R}_2(u)=\{x_1',\ldots,x_{\mu_2}'\}$ and let $t'=h_1t$. Suppose
        $t'((i_q',j_q'))=x_q'$. If $i_q'\geq 2$ then let $y_q'=t'((2,j_q'))$,
        otherwise set $y_q'=x_q'$.
        Define $h_2$ similarly as above, i.e.\ 
        \[
            h_2= (x_1'\,y_1')(x_2'\,y_2')\cdots (x_{\mu_2}'\,y_{\mu_2}')\qquad\in C_{t'}=C_t
        \] 
        where $(x_i'\,y_i')=\text{id}$ if $x_i'=y_i'$. Again, we have 
        \begin{align*}
            \mathsf{R}_2(u)=\{x_1',\ldots,x_{\mu_2}'\} = \{h_2y_q'\;|\;
                           i_q'=1\}\sqcup \{h_2y_q'\;|\; i_q'\geq 2\}  \subseteq
                   \mathsf{R}_1(h_2t')\sqcup\mathsf{R}_2(h_2t').
        \end{align*}
        Hence
        $\bigsqcup_{i=1}^\ell \mathsf{R}_i(u)\subseteq \bigsqcup_{i=1}^\ell
        \mathsf{R}_i(h_2h_1t)$ for $\ell=1,2$. Continuing inductively, the
        statement follows. 
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}


\Cref{prop:dominancecharacterisation} then follows, since for any $t\in
\Delta^\lambda$ and any $i \in \{1,\ldots, (\lambda')_1\}$, we have
$\lambda_i=|\mathsf{R}_i(t)|$ by definition. This hard-earned result translates
to the following test.

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:dominancebyhomextension}
    For $\lambda,\mu\vdash n$, suppose $\varphi\in
    \Hom_{\sym{n}}(S^\lambda, M^\mu)$ is non-zero. If there exists a $\wt\varphi\in
    \Hom_{\sym{n}}(M^\lambda, M^\mu)$ extending $\varphi$, then $\lambda
    \unrhd \mu$. If furthermore $\lambda=\mu$, then $\varphi$ is a scalar
    multiple of the natural inclusion $S^\lambda\into M^\lambda$. 

    \begin{proof}
        We know $S^\lambda$ is cyclic, generated by $e_t$ for any $t\in
        \Delta^\lambda$. Thus $\varphi(e_t)\neq 0$, since $\varphi$ is non-zero.
        For any such $t\in \Delta^\lambda$, if $\wt\varphi$ is as given, then \[0\neq \varphi(e_t)=\wt\varphi(e_t) =
        \wt\varphi(\kappa_t\{t\}) = \kappa_t\wt\varphi(\{t\}).\]
        Write $\wt\varphi(\{t\})$ as a sum of $\mu$-tabloids to see that there
        exists a $u\in \Delta^\mu$ such that $\kappa_t\{u\}\neq 0$. We can then
        conclude by the previous proposition.

        Lastly if $\lambda=\mu$ then $\kappa_t (\wt \varphi\{t\})$ lies in $\bbC
        e_t$ by~\cref{prop:kappaaction}. So $\wt\varphi(e_t)=\varphi(e_t)$ is a scalar multiple of $e_t$ for any $t$.
        Since $\sym{n}$ acts transitively on $e_t$s, it must be the same scalar
        for all $e_t$s.
    \end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
    Not all maps $S^\lambda\to M^\mu$ can be
    extended to $M^\lambda$. Indeed, suppose $\text{char}(\bbk)=2$, and consider the partitions $\lambda=(1^2)$ and
    $\mu=(2)$ of $n=2$. We show that $\Hom_{\sym{2}}(S^\lambda, M^\mu)\neq 0$,
    but for any $\varphi\in \Hom(M^\lambda, M^\mu)$, the restriction
    $\varphi|_{s^\lambda}$ vanishes.  To see the first claim, note that $S^{\lambda}$ is the sign representation, which over a
    field of characteristic two coincides with $\bbOne_{\sym{2}} = M^{\mu}$.
    To see the second, note we have
    $M^\lambda=\left\langle \smalltabloid{1,2}, \smalltabloid{2,1}\right\rangle$, so if
    $\varphi\colon M^\lambda\to M^\mu$ is $\sym{2}$-linear then
    $\varphi(\smalltabloid{1,2})=\varphi((1\,2)\cdot \smalltabloid{2,1})=(1\,
    2)\cdot \varphi(\smalltabloid{2,1})=\varphi(\smalltabloid{2,1})$, and hence
    $\varphi(e_{\smalltableau{1,
    2}})=\varphi(\smalltabloid{1,2})-\varphi(\smalltabloid{2,1})=0$ as required.
\end{example}

\marginpar{Lecture 8, 4/11/26} 
\begin{corollary}
    If $\textnormal{char}(\bbk)=0$ and $\lambda,\mu\vdash n$, then
    $S^\lambda\simeq S^\mu$ if and only if $\lambda = \mu$.
    \begin{proof}
        If there is an isomorphism $S^\lambda\to S^\mu$, then
        composing it with the inclusion of $S^\mu$ into $M^\mu$ gives a non-zero
        $\sym n$-linear map $\varphi\colon S^\lambda\to M^\mu$. By Maschke's
        theorem there is a decomposition $M^\lambda=S^\lambda\oplus V$, so
        $\varphi$ can be extended to a map $\wt\varphi\colon M^\lambda\to M^\mu$
        by setting $\wt\varphi(V)=0$. Thus $\lambda\unrhd \mu$, and by symmetry
        $\mu\unrhd \lambda$.
    \end{proof}
\end{corollary}

We have thus shown that, over a field of characteristic zero, the Specht modules
are irreducible and pairwise non-isomorphic. If in addition $\bbk = \bbC$, then the
number of irreducible representations coincides with the number of conjugacy
classes, which is the number of partitions of $n$ (by disjoint-cycle decomposition). 

We deduce the following.

\begin{corollary}
    The Specht modules $\{S^\lambda\;|\; \lambda\vdash n\}$ form the complete set of irreducible representations
    of $\sym n$ over $\bbC$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
    In fact for $\textnormal{char}(\bbk)=0$ (i.e.\ in \emph{ordinary}
    representation theory), and even in characteristic $p>n$, the Specht modules
    continue to form a complete set of irreducible $\sym{n}$-representations.
    This is however harder, and requires a discussion on \emph{splitting
    fields}.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}
    For each $\lambda\vdash n$, the $\bbC \sym n$-module $M^\lambda$ has an
    irreducible decomposition of the form
    \[
        M^\lambda\simeq S^\lambda \oplus \;\bigoplus_{\mu\underset{\neq}{\rhd}
        \lambda}m_{\lambda\mu}\cdot S^\mu.
    \] 
    \begin{proof}
        If $S^\mu$ is a summand of $M^\lambda$, then we can
        extend the injection $S^\mu\into M^\lambda$ to a non-zero homomorphism
        $M^\mu \to M^\lambda$ by choosing (using Maschke's theorem) an $\sym
        n$-invariant complement $V$ of $S^\mu$ in $M^\mu$ and setting the
        homomorphism to zero on $V$. Thus $\mu \unrhd \lambda$
        by~\cref{thm:dominancebyhomextension}. Furthermore if $\lambda=\mu$,
        then the same theorem shows any map $S^\lambda\to M^\lambda$ is a scalar
        multiple of the natural inclusion, so $\dim(\Hom_{\sym
        n}(S^\lambda,M^\lambda))=1$. It follows, by Schur's lemma, that
        $S^\lambda$ occurs in $M^\lambda$ with multiplicity $1$.
    \end{proof}
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
    Computing the coefficients $m_{\lambda\mu}$ in the above decomposition is a
    question of moderate difficulty, and is answered by \emph{Young's rule}. A
    discussion is beyond the scope of this course.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{A teaser for modular representation theory.} In \emph{modular}
representation theory, i.e.\ when working over characteristic $p$ dividing $|\sym{n}|$, it is
possible that the Specht modules are not irreducible, and we can have
$S^\lambda\simeq S^\mu$ for $\lambda\neq\mu$  --- for example, $S^{(n)}$
and $S^{(1^n)}$ are both the trivial representation in characteristic $2$.

\begin{example}
    Work over $\bbk = \bbF_2$, and take $\lambda=(n-1,1)$ for $n>2$ even. Then
    the standard representation $S^{(n-1,1)}$ is reducible. Indeed,
    $S^{(n-1,1)}$ (over any field) is spanned by polytabloids of the form 
    \[
        e_{ij}=e\left(\tableau{{$i$}{...},{$j$}}\right) = \tabloid{1\ldots
        {$j$-1}{}{$j$+1}\ldots {$n$}, {$j$}}- \tabloid{1\ldots
        {$i$-1}{}{$i$+1}\ldots {$n$}, {$i$}}
    \]
    but over $\bbF_2$ the element $\sum_{i<j}e_{ij}$ is simply $(n-1)$ times the
    sum of all $(n-1,1)$-tabloids and is therefore non-zero and $\sym
    n$-invariant. 
\end{example}

\begin{exercise} 
    Generalise the above argument to show that $S^{(n-1,1)}$ is an irreducible
    $\bbk \sym{n}$-module if and only if $\textnormal{char}(\bbk)$ does not
    divide $n$.
\end{exercise}

It is however true that the
irreducible representations all have the form $S^\lambda/(S^\lambda\cap
(S^\lambda)\orth)$ for \emph{$p$-regular} partitions $\lambda\vdash n$ in the
below sense. 

\begin{definition}
    Given a prime $p>0$, a partition $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ is
    \emph{$p$-singular} if it has $p$ or more equal parts, i.e.\ if
    $\lambda_{j+1}=\lambda_{j+2}=\cdots = \lambda_{j+p}$ for some
    $j\in\{0,\ldots, r-p\}$. We say $\lambda$ is \emph{$p$-regular} if it is not
    $p$-singular.
\end{definition}

More generally, an element $g$ in a finite group $G$ is said to be $p$-regular
if $p$ does not divide the order of $g$. Thus $g\in \sym n$ is $p$-regular if
and only if its disjoint-cycle decomposition has no cycles of length divisible
by $p$. A straightforward combinatorial argument shows that the number of such cycle types
is equal to the number of $p$-regular partitions of $n$.

Then the classification of irreducible representations follows from Brauer's
theorem which we now state.

\begin{theorem}[(Brauer)]
    Over a field $\bbk$ of characteristic $p>0$, and for a finite group $G$, the
    number of absolutely irreducible $\bbk G$-modules is at most the number of
    $p$-regular conjugacy classes in $G$. If furthermore $\bbk$ is a
    \emph{splitting field} for $G$ (i.e.\ if every irreducible $\bbk G$-module
    is absolutely irreducible), then equality holds.
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
    The field $\bbF_p$ is a splitting field for every $p$-group $G$. On the other
    hand, the only $p$-regular conjugacy class in such a group is trivial. Thus
    the only irreducible $\bbF_pG$-module is $\bbOne_G$.
\end{example}

\section{Standard bases of Specht modules}
\marginpar{Lecture 9, 9/11/26}
We will now find particularly a nice basis for each Specht module $S^\lambda$.
We know, of course, that $S^\lambda$ is spanned by polytabloids $e_t$ for $t\in
\Delta^\lambda$. We will narrow down the tableaux $t$ required, and this will be
uniform over all fields $\bbk$. In particular, the dimension of $S^\lambda$ will
turn out to be independent of the field we work over!

\begin{definition}
    Given $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)\vdash n$ and $t\in
    \Delta^\lambda$, we say $t$ is \emph{row-standard} if the entries of $t$
    increase along rows (i.e.\ $t((i,j))<t((i,j+1))$ for all $i,j$). Likewise, we say
    $t$ is \emph{column-standard} if the entries of $t$ increase along columns
    (i.e.\ $t((i,j))<t((i+1,j))$ for all $i,j$). We say $t$, is \emph{standard}
    if it is both row- and column-standard, and write $\text{Std}(\lambda)$ for
    the set of all standard $\lambda$-tableaux.
\end{definition}

Call a polytabloid $e_t$ standard if $t$ is standard. We wish to show that the
set $\{e_t\;|\; t\in \text{Std}(\lambda)\}$ of standard polytabloids is a
$\bbk$-basis of $S^\lambda$.

\begin{example}
    We have $\text{Std}((n))=\{\smalltableau{123{...}}\}$, this is consistent
    with the fact that $S^{(n)}=\bbOne$ is one-dimensional. Likewise there is
    only one standard $(1^n)$-tableau, corresponding to a basis of
    $S^{(1^n)}=\textnormal{sgn}$.

    There are only two standard $(2,1)$-tableaux, namely $\smalltableau{12,3}$
    and $\smalltableau{13,2}$. More generally there are only $n-1$ standard
    $(n-1,1)$-tableaux, since we have
    \[\textnormal{Std}((n-1,1))=\left\{\tableau{12{...}{$\hat{\imath}$}{...}{$n$},{$i$}}\;\middle\vert\;
    2\leq i\leq n\right\}.\]
    This is consistent with the fact that $M^{(n-1,1)}$ is the $n$-dimensional
    permutation representation, and $S^{(n-1,1)}$ is the subspace spanned by
    pairwise differences of basis elements.
\end{example}


\paragraph{Linear independence.} Before we do anything, it is helpful to order all tabloids.

\begin{definition}
    For $\lambda\vdash n$ and $t,u\in \Delta^\lambda$, consider the set
    $\mathcal{N}=[n]\setminus\bigcup_i \left(\mathsf{R}_i(t)\cap
    \mathsf{R}_i(u)\right)$ of entries not appearing in the same row of $t$ and
    $u$. If $\mathcal{N}\neq \varnothing$ (equivalently, $\{t\}\neq \{u\}$), we
    consider $i,j$ such that $\max(\mathcal{N})\in \mathsf{R}_i(t)\cap
    \mathsf{R}_j(u)$, and say $\{t\}>\{u\}$ if $i>j$. 
\end{definition}

This is a total order on the set $\Omega^\lambda$ of $\lambda$-tabloids,
equivalently, on the set of all row-standard $\lambda$-tableaux. For example,
here is the total order on $\Omega^{(3,2)}$.
\[
\tabloid{345,12} < \tabloid{245,13} < \tabloid{235,14}< \tabloid{234,15}
<\tabloid{145,23}< \tabloid{135,24} <\cdots < \tabloid{123,45}
\]

\begin{exercise}
    What is the maximum element in $\Omega^\lambda$ under this order?
\end{exercise}

\begin{lemma}
    For $\lambda\vdash n$, let $t\in \Delta^\lambda$ be column-standard, and
    $h\in C_t\setminus\{\textnormal{id}\}$. Then $\{ht\}<\{t\}$.

    \begin{proof}
        Since $h$ is nontrivial and $R_t\cap C_t=\{\text{id}\}$, we see that
        $\{ht\}\neq \{t\}$ and thus there is a maximal $y\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ that
        lies in distinct rows of $t$ and $ht$. Suppose $y=t((i,j))$, in
        particular $y\notin \mathsf{R}_i(ht)$. Now
        $\mathsf{C}_j(t)=\mathsf{C}_j(ht)$, so by maximality of $y$ and
        column-standardness of $t$, we have $t((i',j))=(ht)((i',j))$ for all
        $i'>i$. Thus $y$ must occur before the $i$th row in $ht$, showing
        $\{t\}>\{ht\}$ as required.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}
    For $\lambda\vdash n$, the collection $\{e_t\;|\; t\in
    \textnormal{Std}(\lambda)\}$ is a linearly independent subset of
    $S^\lambda$.
    \begin{proof}
        Suppose not, so that there is a dependence relation $\sum_{t\in
        \text{Std}(\lambda)} \alpha_te_t=0$ with some $\alpha_t\neq 0$. Write
        $\delta\subseteq\text{Std}(\lambda)$ for the set of $t$ for which
        $\alpha_t\neq 0$. Since
        the standard tableau inherit the total order of the row-standard ones,
        there is a unique maximal $u\in \delta$. Then writing
        $\alpha_{ht}=\sgn(h)\cdot \alpha_t$, we have 
        \[
            0 = \sum_{t\in \delta}\alpha_t e_t = \alpha_u \cdot \{u\} +
            \left(\sum_{h\in C_u\setminus \{\textnormal{id}\}} \alpha_{hu}\cdot
            \{hu\} + \sum_{t\in\delta}\sum_{h\in C_t} \alpha_{ht}\cdot \{ht\}
            \right). 
        \]
        But note that the above lemma (and maximality of $u$) shows each tabloid
        appearing in the second summand is strictly less than $\{u\}$. This
        yields a contradiction, since tabloids form a basis of $M^\lambda$.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Spanning the Specht module.} To show standard polytabloids span
$S^\lambda$, we examine elements of $\bbk \sym n$ that annihilate a given $e_t$.

\begin{definition}
    \label{defn:Garnir}
    For $\lambda\vdash n$ and $t\in \Delta^\lambda$, and two subsets $X\subseteq
    \mathsf{C}_j(t)$ and $Y\subseteq \mathsf{C}_{j+1}(t)$ for $j\in
    \{1,2,\ldots,\lambda_1-1\}$, let $\mathcal{T}$
    denote a set of left coset representatives for $\text{Sym}(X)\times
    \text{Sym}(Y)$ in $\text{Sym}(X\sqcup Y)$. The \emph{Garnir element} defined
    by $X$, $Y$, and $\mathcal{T}$ is \[G_{X,Y}=\sum_{\sigma\in
    \mathcal{T}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot \sigma \qquad \in \bbk\sym n.\]
\end{definition}

Pictorially, we denote $X$ and $Y$ by shading the Young diagram of $t$:
\[\tableau{3{*(Aquamarine) 4}6,{*(Salmon) 2} {*(Aquamarine) 5},{*(Salmon)
1}}\qquad\text{denotes}\qquad t=\tableau{346,25,1}\,,\; j=1,\; X=\{2,1\},\;
Y=\{4,5\}.\]

\begin{example}
    Let the $(2,1)$-tableau $t$ with its subsets $X,Y$ be given as
    $\smalltableau{{*(Salmon) 1}{*(Aquamarine) 2},{*(Salmon) 3}}$, and choose $\mathcal{T}=\{\text{id}, (1\,2), (2\,3)\}$. Then we have
    $G_{X,Y}=1-(1\,2)-(2,3)$. Observe that this annihilates $e_t$ --- 
    \[
        G_{X,Y}\cdot e_t = \left(1-(1\,2)-(2\,3)\right)\cdot \left(\smalltabloid{12,3}-\smalltabloid{32,1}\right)
        = \left(\smalltabloid{12,3}-\smalltabloid{32,1}\right) -
        \left(\smalltabloid{21,3}-\smalltabloid{31,2}\right) -
        \left(\smalltabloid{13,2}-\smalltabloid{23,1}\right) = 0
    \]
\end{example}

\marginpar{Lecture 10, 11/11/26} 
Amazingly, the behaviour in the above example is typical.

\begin{proposition}[(Peel, '75)]
    Let $\lambda,t,j,X,Y$, and $\mathcal{T}$ be as in~\cref{defn:Garnir}, and
    suppose $|X|+|Y| > \lambda'_j$ (i.e.\ $X\cup Y$ contains more
    elements than the $j$th column of $t$). Then $G_{XY}\cdot e_t=0$.

    \begin{proof}
        Define the element \[G_{X\cup Y}=\smashoperator[r]{\sum_{\rho\in
            \text{Sym}(X\cup Y)}}\;\; \sgn(\rho)\cdot
        \rho\qquad \in \bbk \sym n\]
        and note that since any $\rho\in \text{Sym}(X\cup Y)$ can be uniquely
        written as a product $\tau\sigma_1\sigma_2$ for $\tau\in\mathcal{T}$,
        $\sigma_1\in \text{Sym}(X)$, $\sigma_2\in \text{Sym}(Y)$, we can rewrite
        the above as 
        \[
            G_{X\cup Y}= 
            \Bigg( \underbrace{\sum_{\tau\in
            \mathcal{T}}\sgn(\tau)\cdot \tau}_{G_{X,Y}} \Bigg) \cdot
            \Bigg(\smashoperator[r]{\sum_{\sigma_1\in
            \text{Sym}(X)}}\;\;\sgn(\sigma_1)\cdot \sigma_1\Bigg) \cdot
            \Bigg(\smashoperator[r]{\sum_{\sigma_2\in
            \text{Sym}(Y)}}\;\;\sgn(\sigma_2)\cdot \sigma_2\Bigg). 
        \]
        By~\cref{lem:polytabloidaction}, for all $\sigma\in C_t$ (in particular
        for $\sigma\in \text{Sym}(X)\times \text{Sym}(Y)$) we have $\sigma\cdot
        e_t = \sgn(\sigma)\cdot e_t$, and hence $G_{X\cup Y}\cdot e_t =
        |\text{Sym}(X)|\cdot |\text{Sym}(Y)|\cdot G_{X,Y}\cdot e_t$. 
        
        In
        particular it suffices to show that $G_{X\cup Y}\cdot e_t=0$ --- over a
        field of characteristic $0$ it immediately implies $G_{X, Y}\cdot
        e_t=0$; and since $G_{X,Y}\cdot e_t$ is a $\bbZ$-linear combination of
        tabloids, we can reduce modulo $p$ to see that the $\bbF_p$-linear
        combination $(G_{X,Y}\cdot e_t \mod{p})$ also vanishes and hence
        $G_{X,Y}\cdot e_t=0$ over all fields.

        To show $G_{X\cup Y}\cdot e_t=0$, note that since $|X|+|Y|>\lambda'_j$,
        for any $\sigma\in C_t$ there exist entries $a\in X$ and $b\in Y$ that
        lie in the same row of $\sigma t$. Pick a left transversal $\mathcal{Z}$
        of the subgroup $\langle (a\,b)\rangle\subset \text{Sym}(X\cup Y)$, so
        that 
        \[
            G_{X\cup Y}\cdot \{\sigma t\} =
            \sum_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}}\sgn(\zeta)\cdot 
            \zeta \cdot \underbrace{(1-(a\,b))\cdot \{\sigma t\}}_{0} = 0.
        \]
        Since $e_t$ is simply a signed sum of elements of the form $\{\sigma t\}$ for
        $\sigma \in C_t$, the conclusion follows.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

In practice, we choose the set of coset representatives 
$\mathcal{T}=\{\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r\}$ in a way that each $t_i\coloneqq \sigma_i t$
agrees with $t$ away from the positions occupied by $X\cup Y$, and has entries
increasing vertically downwards in the positions occupied by $X\cup Y$. For
example, for $(t,X,Y)$ given by
$\smalltableau{1{*(Aquamarine)2},{*(Salmon)4}{*(Aquamarine)3},{*(Salmon)5}}$, we choose
$\mathcal{T}$ in a way that ascertains 
\[
    t_1=\tableau{12,43,5}, \quad t_2=\tableau{12,34,5},\quad
    t_3=\tableau{12,35,4}, \quad t_4=\tableau{13,24,5},\quad
    t_5=\tableau{13,25,4}, \quad t_6=\tableau{14,25,3}.
\]
That is, we choose $\mathcal{T}=\{\text{id}, (3\,4),(3\,5\,4),
(2\,3\,4),(2\,3\,5\,4),(2\,4)(3\,5)\}$, to obtain the Garnir element
\[G_{X,Y}=1-(3\,4)+(3\,5\,4)+(2\,3\,4)+(2\,3\,5\,4)+(2\,4)(3\,5).\]
Thus $G_{X,Y}\cdot e_t=e_{t_1}-e_{t_2}+e_{t_3}+e_{t_4}-e_{t_5}+e_{t_6}=0$, and
in particular, $e_t = e_{t_2}-e_{t_3}+e_{t_4}+e_{t_5}-e_{t_6}.$
Note we started with a non-standard polytabloid, and expressed it as a linear
combination of standard ones! This process is called \emph{straightening} of the
polytabloid.

\medskip

We now show that all polytabloids can be straightened.

Given $\lambda\vdash n$ and two column-standard tableaux $t,u\in
\Delta^\lambda$, write $\mathcal{B}=[n]\setminus \bigcup_{i}(\mathsf{C}_i(t)\cap
\mathsf{C}_j(t))$ for the set of entries not in the same
column of $u$ and $t$. Since $t,u$ are both column-standard, we have $\sigma
\cdot t\neq u$ for all $\sigma\in C_t$, and thus 
$\mathcal{B}$ is non-empty and (analogously to how the order on tabloids
was defined) we can consider $i,j$ such that $\max(\mathcal{B})\in
\mathsf{C}_i(t)\cap \mathsf{C}_j(u)$, and say $t \gg u$ if $i>j$. This is again
a total order on column-standard tableaux.

\begin{proposition}
    For $\lambda\vdash n$, let $v\in \Delta^\lambda$ be column-standard. Then $e_v$ lies in the $\bbk$-span of standard
    polytabloids.
    \begin{proof}
        Write $W$ for the $\bbk$-span of standard polytabloids. Let the
        column-standard $\lambda$-tableaux be enumerated as $t_1\gg t_2\gg
        \cdots$; we first prove by induction on $r$ that each $e_{t_r}$ lies
        in $W$. The statement for $r=1$ is verified since the $(\gg)$-maximal
        tableau is evidently standard (\emph{exercise}: write it down explicitly).  

        \marginpar{Lecture 11, 16/11/26} 
        To address the case $t=t_r$, suppose we know $e_u\in W$ for all
        column-standard $u\gg t$. If $t$ is also row-standard then there is
        nothing to prove. Otherwise, there exist $i,j$ such that
        $t((i,j))>t((i,j+1))$. Define $X=\{t((\ell,j))\;|\; i\leq \ell\leq
        \lambda'_j\}$, and $Y=\{t((\ell,j+1))\;|\; 1\leq \ell\leq i\}$.
        \[
            \tableau{{}{}{*(Aquamarine) }{}{}, 
                     {}{}{*(Aquamarine) \raisebox{1em}{$\wedge$}}{}, 
                     {}{*(Salmon)}{*(Aquamarine) \raisebox{1em}{$\wedge$}}{\hspace{-3.3em}{$>$}}, 
                     {}{*(Salmon) \raisebox{1em}{$\wedge$}}{},{}{*(Salmon) \raisebox{1em}{$\wedge$}}{},
                     {}}
        \]

        Choose a transversal $\mathcal{T}$ of
        $\text{Sym}(X)\times\text{Sym}(Y)\subseteq \text{Sym}(X\cup Y)$ such that
        $\text{id}\in \mathcal{T}$. Then we have \[0=G_{X,Y}\cdot e_t = e_t +
            \smashoperator[r]{\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{T}\setminus
            \{\text{id}\}}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot
        \underbrace{\sigma\cdot e_t}_{e_{\sigma t}} \]
        whence it suffices to prove $e_{\sigma t}\in W$ for all $\sigma\in \mathcal{T}\setminus \{\text{id}\}$. For such $\sigma$, note that
        $\sigma\notin \text{Sym}(X)\times\text{Sym}(Y)$ and thus the sets $X\cap
        \sigma(Y)$ and $Y\cap \sigma(X)$ are non-empty.

        Let $u$ be the unique column-standard $\lambda$-tableau satisfying
        $\mathsf{C}_\ell(u)=\mathsf{C}_\ell(\sigma t)$ for all $\ell$, and
        consider
        \[
            \mathcal{B}=[n]\setminus \bigcup_\ell (\mathsf{C}_\ell(t)\cap
            \mathsf{C}_\ell(u)).
        \] 
        This is equal to $(X\cap \sigma(Y))\sqcup (Y\cap \sigma(X))$. In
        particular $\mathcal{B}$ is non-empty, and 
        its maximal element $\max(\mathcal{B})$ evidently lies in
        $X\cap \sigma(Y)\subseteq \mathsf{C}_j(t)\cap \mathsf{C}_{j+1}(u)$. Thus
        $u\gg t$, and by induction hypothesis, $e_u\in W$. But $u = \sigma'^{-1} \sigma t$ for some $\sigma'\in C_{\sigma t}$, so that $e_{\sigma
        t}=e_{\sigma'u} = \text{sgn}(\sigma')\cdot e_u$ also lies in $W$ as required.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[(Standard basis theorem)]
    For $\lambda\vdash n$, the set of standard polytabloids $\{e_t\;|\; t\in
    \textnormal{Std}(\lambda)\}$ forms a basis for the Specht
    module $S^\lambda$ over any field $\bbk$.

    \begin{proof}
        We have checked that the standard polytabloids are linearly independent.
        To show they span $S^\lambda$, note that for any $t\in \Delta^\lambda$
        there exists a $\sigma\in C_t$ such that $u=\sigma^{-1} t$ is column
        standard, and $e_u$ lies in the span of standard polytabloids by the the
        above proposition. But then so does $e_t = e_{\sigma u} =
        \sgn(\sigma)\cdot e_u$.
    \end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
    The standard basis is \emph{not} a permutation basis in general. Indeed we
    know $\sigma\cdot e_t = e_{\sigma t}$ for all $\sigma\in \sym n$ and all
    $t\in \Delta^\lambda$, but there are many $\sigma, t$ for which $t$ is
    standard and $\sigma$ is not.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    It can be deduced from the above proofs that over a field of characteristic
    zero, any polytabloid can be written as a $\bbZ$-linear combination of
    standard polytabloids. Furthermore, the matrices representing any $\sigma\in
    \sym n$ with respect to the standard basis all have integer entries.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}
    The $\bbk \sym n$-modules $S^\lambda$ have the same dimension over any
    field $\bbk$, and this is given by the number of standard
    $\lambda$-tableaux.
\end{corollary}

\begin{example}
    There are $\binom{4}{2}=6$ standard $(3,1,1)$-tableaux. To see this, note that
    the top right entry must be $1$, and the remaining two entries of the first
    row (taken out of $\{2,3,4,5\}$) determine the rest of the tableau. Thus $S^{(3,1,1)}$ is always $6$-dimensional.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Hooks.} Work over $\bbC$, and for $\lambda\vdash n$ write
$\chi^\lambda$ for the character of the Specht module $S^\lambda$. We will
provide a closed formula for computing $\chi^\lambda(1)=\dim S^\lambda$, by
breaking $\lambda$ into $\Gamma$-shaped subtableaux (i.e.\ subtableaux of shape
$(m,1,1,\ldots, 1)$) called \emph{hook}.

\begin{definition}
    Given $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)\vdash n$ and $1\leq i\leq
    \lambda'_1$, $1\leq j\leq \lambda_i$, the
    \emph{$(i,j)$-hook} of $\lambda$ is 
    \[
        h_{i,j}^\lambda = \{(i,j)\}\; \sqcup\; \{(i,y)\;|\; j<y\leq \lambda_i\} \;
        \sqcup \; \{(x,j)\;|\; i<x\leq \lambda'_j\}.
    \]
    The three pieces in the above decomposition are called the \emph{corner},
    the \emph{arm}, and the \emph{leg} of the hook respectively. We say the
    extremal boxes $(i,\lambda_i)$ and $(\lambda'_j,j)$ are the \emph{hand} and
    the \emph{foot} of the hook respectively.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    Take the partition $(8,6,5,4,2,1)\vdash 26$, and consider the $(2,3)$-hook
    shown below.
    The corner is shaded black ($\smalltableau{{*(Black)}}$), the arm is shaded
    green ($\smalltableau{{*(Aquamarine)}}$), and the leg is shaded pink
    ($\smalltableau{{*(Salmon)}}$). The hand and foot are labelled `h' and `f' respectively.
    \[
        \tableau{
            {}{}{}{}{}{}{}{},
            {}{}{*(Black)}{*(Aquamarine)}{*(Aquamarine)}{*(Aquamarine)h},
            {}{}{*(Salmon)}{}{},
            {}{}{*(Salmon)f}{},
            {}{},
            {}
        }
    \]
\end{example}

We write $\ell_{ij}^\lambda = \lambda_i+\lambda_{j}'-i-j+1$ for the
\emph{length} (i.e.\ size) of the $(i,j)$-hook of $\lambda$. We also write
$\mathcal{H}(\lambda)=\{\ell^\lambda_{ij}\}$ for the \emph{multi}set of all
hook-lengths of $\lambda$ (this includes counts of repetitions of the same hook
length). This can be conveniently encoded as the \emph{hook graph}, which
replaces the $(i,j)$th box in the Young diagram with $\ell^{\lambda}_{ij}$. 
\[
    \tableau{
            {13}{11}{9}{8}{6}{4}{2}{1},
            {10}{8}{6}{5}{3}{1},
            {8}{6}{4}{3}{1},
            {6}{4}{2}{1},
            {3}{1},
            {1}
    }
\]

\begin{theorem}[(Hook length formula (Frame, Robinson, Thrall, '53))]  
    For $\lambda\vdash n$, we have \[\chi^\lambda(1)=\frac{n!}{\prod_{\ell\in
    \mathcal{H}(\lambda)}\ell}.\]
\end{theorem}

This is surprising, there is a priori no reason to expect even the fact that the
product of all hook lengths divides $n!$ ! There are multiple proofs of
different flavours (probabilistic, combinatorial, etc.) but we won't look at
them.

\marginpar{Lecture 12, 18/11/26} 
\begin{example}
    If $\lambda=(n)$, then the hook lengths are $n,n-1, \ldots,2,1$, so we find
    $\chi^{(n)}=n!/(n\cdot n-1 \cdots 2\cdot 1)=1$ which is reassuring. For
    small partitions like $\lambda=(3,2)$, which has hook graph
    $\smalltableau{431,21}$, we can again verify that $5!/(4\cdot 3\cdot 1\cdot
    2\cdot 1)=5$ is the cardinality of the set of standard tableau
    \[\text{Std}((3,2))=\left\{\tableau{123,45} \,,\, \tableau{124,35} \,,\,
    \tableau{125,34} \,,\, \tableau{134,25} \,,\, \tableau{135,24}\right\}.\]
    For larger examples like $(8,6,5,4,2,1)\vdash 26$, it will perhaps take more
    than an hour to list all 1,366,443,828,750 ($=26!/(13\cdot 11 \cdot 9 \cdots
    3 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 )$) standard tableau.
\end{example}

\section{Induction and restriction}
There are many, many combinatorial algorithms to compute various tensor
products, inductions, and restrictions of the Specht modules. We will briefly
look at one of these, namely, the \emph{branching rule}.

Work over $\bbC$. How do $\sym n$-modules relate to $\sym {n-1}$-modules?
Recall, if $V_n$ is the natural $n$-dimensional permutation representation of
$\sym n$, then $(V_n)\downarrow^{\sym n}_{\sym {n-1}}\simeq V_{n-1}\oplus
\bbOne_{\sym {n-1}}$. The branching rule computes analogous restrictions for all
irreducible representations $S^\lambda$.

\begin{theorem}[(Branching rule)]
    \label{thm:branchingrule}
    For $\lambda\vdash n$, we have $(S^\lambda)\!\!\downarrow^{\sym n}_{\sym
    {n-1}}\simeq \bigoplus_{\mu} S^\mu$, where the direct sum is over all
    $\mu\vdash (n-1)$ that can be obtained by removing a single box from the
    Young diagram of $\lambda$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
    What about the more general problem of restricting the
    $\sym{n}$-representation $(S^\lambda)$ to $\sym{n-r}\times \sym{r}$? The
    \emph{Littlewood--Richardson rule} 
    is an algorithm (analogous to the branching rule) for calculating this; the
    result has gained notoriety because of the number of fallacious proofs there
    are of it. There is a great quotation due to Gordon James about it, he says
    that ``Unfortunately the Littlewood--Richardson rule is much harder to prove
    than was at first suspected. I was once told that the Littlewood--Richardson
    rule helped to get men on the moon, but was not proved until after they got
    there.''
\end{remark}

Proving the branching rule is, fortunately, simpler than proving the
Littlewood--Richardson rule. We discuss a proof due to Peel.

\begin{proof}[Proof of~\cref{thm:branchingrule}]
    Let $w_1<\cdots < w_r$ be integers such that a box can be removed from the
    $w_i$th row of $[\lambda]$ to leave a Young diagram (see example below), and write $\mu^i\vdash
    (n-1)$ for the partition thus obtained.
    \[{
        \tableau{
            {\hspace{-5em}{$w_1=1$}}{}{}{}{*(Salmon)},
            {\hspace{-5em}{$w_2=2$}}{}{}{*(Salmon)},
            {}{}{},
            {\hspace{-5em}{$w_3=4$}}{}{*(Salmon)},
            {*(Salmon) \hspace{-5em}{$w_4=5$}}
        }
    }\]
    
    Write $F^iS^\lambda$ for the span of all standard polytabloids $e_t$ such
    that $n\in \bigcup_{j\leq i} \mathsf{R}_{w_j}(t)$, and note that this is
    $\sym {n-1}$-invariant. Thus we have a filtration of $\bbC \sym
    {n-1}$-modules \[0=F^0S^\lambda\subset F^1S^\lambda \subset \cdots \subset F^rS^\lambda = (S^\lambda)\downarrow_{\sym {n-1}}^{\sym n}.\]
    Claim that the $i$th subquotient $F^i S^\lambda / F^{i-1}S^\lambda$ is
    precisely $S^{\mu^i}$, the result clearly follows from this since $\bbC \sym
    {n-1}$ is a semisimple algebra. To prove the claim, we define a map
    \begin{align*}
        \vartheta_i\colon (M^\lambda)\downarrow_{\sym {n-1}}^{\sym
        n}\longrightarrow M^{\mu ^ i}, \qquad 
        \vartheta_i\{t\} = \begin{cases}
            \{\ol t\}, &n\in \mathsf{R}_{w_i}(t) \\
            0, &n\notin \mathsf{R}_{w_i}(t) \\ 
        \end{cases}
    \end{align*}
    where $\ol t$ is obtained by removing $n$ from $t$. Note $\{\ol t\}$ in this
    case remains a tabloid since the $w_i$ were precisely defined such that the
    strict inequality $|\mathsf{R}_{w_i}(t)| >
    |\mathsf{R}_{w_i+1}(t)|$ holds. Likewise if $t$ is standard, then $\ol t$ is
    also a (standard) tabloid, and we compute that  $\vartheta_i(e_t)=e_{\ol t}$
    if $n\in \mathsf{R}_{w_i}(t)$ and $\vartheta_i(e_t)=0$ otherwise.

    Clearly $F^{i-1}S^\lambda\subseteq \ker\vartheta_i$, so we can refine the
    filtration to obtain 
    \[0=F^0S^\lambda\subseteq (F^1S^\lambda\cap \ker\vartheta_1)\subseteq
    F^1S^\lambda \subseteq (F^2S^\lambda\cap \ker\vartheta_2)\subseteq \cdots
    \subseteq F^rS^\lambda = (S^\lambda)\downarrow_{\sym {n-1}}^{\sym n}.\]
    But it is also clear that $F^iS^\lambda/(F^iS^\lambda\cap \ker
    \theta_i)\simeq \vartheta_i(F^iS^\lambda)$ is isomorphic to $S^{\mu^i}$.
    Furthermore, the standard basis theorem readily shows $\sum_{i=1}^r\dim
    S^{\mu^i}=\dim S^\lambda$, and hence the subquotients $(F^iS^\lambda\cap
    \ker\vartheta_i)/F^{i-1}S^\lambda$ must vanish. The claim is then immediate.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
    We have $(S^{(5,4,3,3,1)})\downarrow_{\sym {16}}^{\sym
    {15}}\quad =S^{(4,4,3,3,1)}\oplus S^{(5,3,3,3,1)}\oplus S^{(5,3,3,2,1)} \oplus
    S^{(5,4,3,3)}.$
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    Let $\mu\vdash (n-1)$ and $\lambda\vdash n$. By Frobenius reciprocity, the
    multiplicity of $S^\lambda$ in $(S^\mu)\uparrow_{\sym {n-1}}^{\sym n}$ is
    equal to that of $S^\mu$ in $(S^\lambda)\downarrow_{\sym {n-1}}^{\sym n}$,
    which is $1$ if $[\mu]$ is contained in $[\lambda]$ and is $0$ otherwise. In
    this form the branching rule is sometimes known as \emph{Pieri's rule}.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{The Young graph.} The branching rules allows for a completely
different approach to examining the Specht modules simultaneously.

\begin{definition} 
    The \emph{Young (branching) graph} $\bbY$ is the graph with vertex set equal
    to the set $\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{P}(n)$ of all partitions, and an edge
    $\lambda$--$\mu$ if the Young diagrams of $\lambda,\mu$ differ by a single
    box. We say $\mathcal{P}(n)$ is the \emph{$n$th layer} of $\bbY$, and a path
    in the graph is \emph{upward} if it starts at $\varnothing$ doesn't visit
    the same layer twice. 
    \[
        \begin{tikzcd}[column sep={2.8em,between origins}, row sep={2.5em,between origins}]
            & & & & & \dots \\
            & & & & \smalltableau{{}{}{}{}} & \\
            & & & \smalltableau{{}{}{}} \arrow[dr, no head] \arrow[ur, no head] & & \dots \\
            & & \smalltableau{{}{}} \arrow[dr, no head] \arrow[ur, no head] & & \smalltableau{{}{}{},{}}  & \\
            \varnothing \arrow[r, no head, very thick] & \smalltableau{{}} \arrow[dr, no
            head, very thick] \arrow[ur, no head] & & \smalltableau{{}{},{}} \arrow[dr, no
            head] \arrow[ur, no head, very thick] \arrow[r, no head] & \smalltableau{{}{},{}{}} & \dots \\
            & & \smalltableau{{},{}} \arrow[dr, no head] \arrow[ur, no head,
            very thick] & & \smalltableau{{}{},{},{}}  & \\
            & & & \smalltableau{{},{},{}} \arrow[dr, no head] \arrow[ur, no head] & & \dots \\
            & & & & \smalltableau{{},{},{},{}} & \\
            & & & & & \dots 
        \end{tikzcd}
    \]
\end{definition}

There is a bijection between standard $\lambda$-tableaux and `upward' (i.e.\
rightward in the above diagram) paths --- for instance the thickened path above
corresponds to the following construction of a standard $(3,1)$-tableau
\[
    \varnothing \longrightarrow \tableau{1} \longrightarrow \tableau{1,2} \longrightarrow
    \tableau{13,2}\longrightarrow \tableau{134,2}.
\]
Thus, for example, we can count dimensions of Specht modules by counting upward
paths terminating in a given vertex. There are other applications of such
diagrammatic calculi that we won't dive into.


\section{On representations of the general linear group}
\marginpar{Lecture 13, 23/11/26} 
Let $G$ be the algebraic group $\mathsf{GL}(n,\bbC)$. Again, a representation of
$G$ on a $\bbC$-vector space $V$ is a group homomorphism $\rho\colon G\to
\mathsf{GL}(V)$; if $V$ is $m$-dimensional then after choosing a basis of $V$
this can equivalently be described as a homomorphism $\rho\colon G\to
\mathsf{GL}(m,\bbC)$.  Now for every non-singular matrix $M=(m_{ij})\in G$, the
matrix $\rho(M)=(\rho_{rs}M)$ is non-singular. These functions $M\mapsto
\rho_{rs}M$ could, in principle, be chaotic. 

\begin{definition}
    An $m$-dimensional representation of $G$ given by $\rho\colon G\to
    \mathsf{GL}(m,\bbC)$ is \emph{algebraic} if the matrix entries $M\mapsto
    \rho_{rs}(M)$ are regular functions on the algebraic variety $G$.
\end{definition}

What does this mean?  View $G=\mathsf{GL}(n,\bbC)$ as an open subvariety of
$\bbA^{n\times n}$, obtained as the complement of the hypersurface $\{M\;|\;\det
M = 0\}$. The coordinate ring of $G$ is thus the localisation
\[\bbC[G]=\bbC[\underbrace{x_{11},x_{12},\ldots,x_{nn}}_{X},y]/(y\cdot
\det X=1)\]
and regular functions on $G$ thus have form $P(X)/(\det X)^r$ for some
polynomial $P$, and some $r\geq 0$. An algebraic representation is one for which
every matrix entry has this form.

Be warned that polynomial representations of $G$ form a \emph{strictly}
smaller subclass of the algebraic ones. 

\begin{example}
    The identity function (defined by the coordinate entries $\rho_{rs} M = m_{rs}$)
    is the \emph{defining} (or \emph{tautological}) representation on $\bbC^n$. Its
    tensor powers $(\bbC^n)^{\otimes r}$ are polynomial, with entries given by
    degree $r$ monomials in matrix entries. Likewise the
    representations $\det^{\pm}\colon M\mapsto \det(M)^\pm$ are algebraic. More
    generally, $M\mapsto (M^{-1})^\mathsf{T}$ is a representation (the dual of
    the tautological representation), where we note that if $n>1$ then the
    entries of $M^{-1}$ are necessarily not polynomials. 
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    The group $\mathsf{GL}(1,\bbC)$, whose
    coordinate ring is $\bbC[t^{\pm 1}]$, has one-dimensional algebraic representations given by monomials $t\mapsto
    t^\lambda$ ($\lambda\in \bbZ$). The choices $\lambda\geq 0$ correspond to
    polynomial representations. More generally, irreducible algebraic representations of
    tori $(\bbC^\times)^n$ can be indexed by dominant integral weights
    $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$ for $\lambda_1\geq \cdots\geq
    \lambda_n$. The polynomial ones correspond precisely to the case
    $\lambda_n\geq 0$.
\end{example}

Note that as an abstract group, $G$ has many representations (which may or
may not be polynomial, analytic, smooth, differentiable, or continuous). For
example, the group $\mathsf{GL}(1,\bbC)$ has a representation given by $z\mapsto
\mat{1 & \log |z| \\ 0 & 1}$, this is smooth but very non-algebraic. By exploiting the fact that $G$ has additional structure of
an algebraic variety, and only allowing homomorphisms $G\to
\mathsf{GL}(m,\bbC)$ that are also morphisms of algebraic varieties, we
obtain a restricted but nicer and more thorough theory.

\begin{theorem}
    Algebraic representations of $G=\mathsf{GL}(n,\bbC)$ are semisimple.
    \begin{proof}
        This is essentially Weyl's unitary trick. Let $U=\mathsf{U}(n,\bbC)$ be
        the unitary subgroup of $G$; this is compact so has a Haar measure $dg$.
        If $V$ is an algebraic representation of $G$ and $\langle -,-
        \rangle^\circ$ is any hermitian form on $V$, then we can average it over
        $U$ to get a non-degenerate $U$-invariant hermitian form \[\langle
        v,w\rangle = \int_{U}\langle gv,gw\rangle^\circ \; dg.\]
        Thus for any $G$-subrepresentation $W\subset V$, we can take the
        orthogonal space $W\orth$ with respect to $\langle-,-\rangle$ to obtain
        a decomposition $V=W\oplus W\orth$, where each summand is
        $U$-stable. 

        Consider the subset of $G$ given by group elements $M$ that preserve
        this decomposition. This is a closed subset; one way to see this is to
        choose bases $\{v_1,\ldots,v_i\}\in W$ and $\{v_{i+1},\ldots,v_n\}\in
        W\orth$, let $\rho_{rs}(M)\in \bbC[G]$ denote the matrix entries for this basis of $V$, and note that $M$ preserves the decomposition if and only if
        the matrix $(\rho_{rs}M)$ has a block diagonal form. Furthermore, this
        closed subset contains the unitary group $U$ by construction. 


        But $U$ is Zariski-dense\footnote{
            Note that $\mathsf{U}(n,\bbC)$ is \emph{not} a complex-algebraic subvariety of $\mathsf{GL}(n,\bbC)$. Here we are simply referring to it as a Zariski-dense sub\emph{set}, in the sense that any regular function on $\mathsf{GL}(n,\bbC)$ that vanishes on unitary matrices necessarily vanishes everywhere.
        } in $G$, so in fact the summands are also $G$-stable and $V$ decomposes. 
    \end{proof}
\end{theorem}

Thus it makes sense to ask, what are irreducible algebraic representations of
$G$?

\begin{lemma}
    Let $V$ be an irreducible algebraic representation of $G$. Then there exists
    an $m\in \bbZ$, $r\in \bbN$, and an irreducible subrepresentation $W$ of
    $(\bbC^n)^{\otimes r}$ such that $V\simeq W\otimes \det^{\otimes m}$.
\end{lemma}
