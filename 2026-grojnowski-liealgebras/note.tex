This is a course about symmetries controlled by the \emph{linear algebraic groups}
\begin{align*}
    \sf{GL}_n(\bbC) &=\{ A\in \Mat{n}(\bbC) \;|\; \det A \neq 0\}, &
    \sf{O}_n(\bbC) &=\{ A\in \Mat{n}(\bbC) \;|\; A^{\sf{T}} \cdot A = \id \}, \\
    \sf{SL}_n(\bbC) &=\{ A\in \Mat{n}(\bbC) \;|\; \det A = 1\}, &
    \sf{SP}_{n}(\bbC) &=\{ A\in \Mat{n}(\bbC) \;|\; \langle x,y \rangle =
    \langle Ax, Ay\rangle \; \forall \, x,y\in \bbC^{n}\}
\end{align*}
(here the \emph{Symplectic group} $\sf{SP}_n(\bbC)$ is defined
when $n$ is even, and the given definition depends on the choice of a
non-degenerate skew-symmetric bilinear form $\langle-,-\rangle\colon
\bbC^n\times \bbC^n\to \bbC$), and five others --- which happen to be groups
equipped with additional structures of a smooth manifold and an algebraic
variety. These structures mean we can use topological and analytic methods to
study the groups; on the other hand the structures also simplify much of the
study of these groups to simple linear algebra. This reduction is accomplished
by associating the group to its tangent space at identity, which comes naturally
equipped with the structure of a \emph{Lie algebra} that shadows the group
operation. 

This course is about Lie algebras and their representation theory over a field of characteristic zero.

\section{Getting started}
\marginpar{Lecture 1, 08/10/26} 

The Lie algebra associated to an algebraic group $G$ is the tangent space
$\frakg=\sf{T}_\id G$ at the identity element. The group structure on $G$ induces
the structure of a \emph{Lie algebra} on $\frakg$. We unpack this with an ad hoc definition first. 

\begin{definition}[Ad hoc] A complex \emph{linear algebraic group} $G$ is a
    subvariety of the affine space $\Mat{n}(\bbC)$ of $n\times n$ matrices,
    defined as the zero locus of some polynomials in matrix coefficients,
    equipped with an associative multiplication map $G\times G\to G$ and an
    inverse map $G\to G$ defined by polynomials in matrix coefficients, such
    that the usual axioms of a group are satisfied.
\end{definition}

This is a terrible definition, because it is not \emph{intrinsic}. It relies on
choosing an embedding of the group into a coordinate system. There \emph{is} an
intrinsic definition, under which a linear algebraic group is simply a
group-object in the category of affine varieties. 

\begin{remark}
    It is not obvious that every such intrinsically defined object can be
    embedded into some affine space $\Mat{n}(\bbC)$, but it follows from noting that
    $G$ would then act on the space of functions on $G$ faithfully, and by
    Hilbert's basis theorem there is a finite dimensional faithful
    sub-representation therein.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    Each linear algebraic group has a maximal compact subgroup (unique up to
    conjugacy), and the linear algebraic group is diffeomorphic to the tangent
    bundle over this compact subgroup. Note that the tangent bundle of any Lie
    group is trivial.

    For instance the maximal compact subgroup of $\sf{SL}_n(\bbC)$ is the special
    unitary group $\sf{SU}_n(\bbC)$, diffeomorphic to an $n$-sphere. Likewise
    the maximal compact subgroup inside $\sf{SO}_n(\bbC)$ is $\sf{SO}_n(\bbR)$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{What is a tangent space?} Let us begin with an example, by
calculating the tangent space to $\sf{SL}_2(\bbC)$ at $\id=\mat{1&0 \\ 0&1}$. 
An element close to $\id$ can be expressed as \[P=\id + \varepsilon \mat{a & b
\\ c &  d} + \varepsilon^2 ( \cdots ),\]
so for this to lie in $\sf{SL}_2(\bbC)$ we must have $\det(P)=1$, which expands
to
\[1+\varepsilon(a+d)+\varepsilon^2(\cdots )=1.\]
To compute the tangent space we wish for $\varepsilon$ to be ``very small'', so
that $\varepsilon^2\approx 0$ and the tangent space is this first-order neighbourhood
\[\sl_2(\bbC) = \{\mat{a&b \\ c&d} \;|\; a+d =0\}.\] 

This construction can be made rigorous by working over the ring
$\bbE=\bbC[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$. Recall that if $G$ is a complex linear
algebraic group and $R$ is a $\bbC$-algebra, we define the \emph{set of
$R$-points of $G$} as 
\[
    G(R)=\{
        P\in \Mat{n}(R) \;|\; 
        P\text{ satisfies the polynomial equations defining } 
        G\subset \Mat{n}(\bbC)
    \}
\]
There is a map $\bbE\to \bbC$ sending $\varepsilon\mapsto 0$, this in turn
induces a map $\pi\colon G(\bbE)\to G(\bbC)$. The tangent space $\frakg=\sf{T}_\id G$
is then defined as the pre-image $\pi^{-1}(\id)$.


\begin{exercise}
    Show that $\sf{GL}_n(\bbC)$ is a linear algebraic group (\emph{hint: the condition
    $\det P \neq 0$ can be written as the vanishing of a polynomial by embedding
    $\sf{GL}_n(\bbC)$ into $\Mat{(n+1)}(\bbC)$}). Use this to show that the
    associated Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{gl}_n(\bbC) =\Mat{n}(\bbC)$.
\end{exercise}
 
\begin{exercise}
    For an $n\times n$ matrix $X$, show that $\det(\id + \varepsilon X) = 1 +
    \varepsilon \cdot \text{tr}(X)$. Conclude that the Lie algebra $\sl_n(\bbC)$
    associated to $\sf{SL}_n(\bbC)$ is the space of traceless $n\times n$
    matrices. 
\end{exercise}

\begin{example}
    For the orthogonal group over $\bbC$, the associated Lie algebra is
    the space of antisymmetric matrices. Note that all such matrices are
    traceless over any field of characteristic $\neq 2$, thus $\sf{SO}(n)$ has
    the same Lie algebra. We therefore denote it as $\so_n(\bbC)$.
\end{example}

In the above example we have learnt that different groups can have the same Lie
algebra. This shouldn't be surprising --- for instance, all finite groups are
linear algebraic groups, and their Lie algebra is the zero algebra $\mathbf{0}$.
In a similar fashion the orthogonal group is disconnected, with two connected
components diffeomorphic to each other, one of which is $\sf{SO}_n(\bbC)$.

One thing we will learn in due course is that a Lie group, its maximal compact
subgroup, and its Lie algebra all have coincident finite dimensional
representations.
 

\paragraph{The Lie bracket.} What structure does $\frakg=\sf{T}_\id G$ have that is
inherited from the group structure on $G$? It is not multiplication. 

What we do instead is to look at the commutator map $G\times G\to G$ sending
$(p,q)\mapsto p^{-1}q^{-1}pq$. This sends $(\id,\id)\mapsto \id$, thus the
derivative of the commutator gives a linear map 
\[
    \frakg\times \frakg \simeq T_{(\id,\id)}(G\times
    G)\longrightarrow \sf{T}_\id G \simeq \frakg.
\]
This is simply the zero map, and is not quite what we want --- we want a
\emph{bilinear} map $\frakg\times \frakg\to \frakg$, so we differentiate
the first and the second variable separately.

To explain the construction, suppose $G$ is $\sf{GL}_n(\bbC)$ or another matrix
group. Let $P=\id + X\varepsilon$, $Q=\id + Y\delta$ where $\varepsilon, \delta$
are small and satisfy $\varepsilon^2=\delta^2=0$ but
$\varepsilon\delta=\delta\varepsilon\neq 0$ (i.e.\ we work over
the ring $\bbk[\varepsilon, \delta]/(\varepsilon^2,\delta^2)$). We then see that
\[
    P^{-1}Q^{-1}PQ=\id + \varepsilon\delta (XY-YX),
\]
so it makes sense to introduce the map
$[\,,\,]\colon \frakg\times
\frakg\to \frakg$ given by $[X,Y]=XY-YX$. This is the \emph{Lie bracket}.

\begin{exercise}
    The above calculation is an extrinsic proof of the fact that the Lie
    bracket $[\,,\,]\colon\frakg\times \frakg \to \frakg$ is \emph{antisymmetric}. To get an intrinsic proof that doesn't rely
    on any embedding into matrix groups, use the fact that
    $(pqp^{-1}q^{-1})^{-1}=qpq^{-1}p^{-1}$ for any group elements $p,q$.
\end{exercise}

The next property of the Lie bracket is the \emph{Jacobi identity}:
\[[[x,y],z]+[[y,z],x] + [[z,x],y]=0 \qquad \text{for all }x,y,z\in \frakg.\] 
We will deduce this over multiple exercises, seeing three reasons why it is
true. Next time we will look at a fourth and a fifth explanation. 

\begin{exercise}
    For $G\subseteq \sf{GL}(n,\bbC)$, show by direct
    computation that the Jacobi identity holds.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
    Marshall Hall discovered the following identity in the 1930s: writing
    $p^q=q^{-1}pq$ and $(p,q)=p^{-1}q^{-1}pq$, Hall showed that in any
    group $G$ with elements $p,q,r$ we have
    \[((p,q^{-1}),r)^q \cdot ((q,r^{-1}),p)^r \cdot ((r,p^{-1}),q)^p=\id.\] 
    Differentiate this to deduce the Jacobi identity.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
    Deduce the Jacobi identity from associativity of the group law on $G$.
\end{exercise}

\paragraph{Lie algebras.} The two properties above --- antisymmetry and the
Jacobi identity --- define an abstract Lie algebra. Fix a field $\bbk$ of
characteristic $\neq 2,3$.

\begin{definition}
    Let $\frakg$ be a $\bbk$-vector space. A \emph{Lie bracket} on $\frakg$ is an
    antisymmetric bilinear form $[\,,\,]\colon \frakg\times\frakg\to \frakg$ that
    satisfies the Jacobi identity. We say $\frakg$ equipped with a Lie bracket is a
    \emph{Lie algebra}.
\end{definition}

\begin{exercise}
    \marginpar{Lecture 2, 10/10/26}
    Here is another way to define a Lie bracket on a $\bbk$-vector
    space $\frakg$ --- it is given by an element $\mu\in \Exter^2(\frakg\dual)\otimes \frakg$
    (i.e.\ a $\frakg$-valued 2-form on $\frakg\dual$) which lies in the kernel of the
    induced exterior derivative $d_\mu \colon \Exter^\bullet(\frakg\dual)\otimes \frakg \to
    \Exter^{\bullet+1}(\frakg\dual)\otimes \frakg$. Recall that this derivative is
    given by $d_\mu(1)=0$, $d_\mu\omega = \omega \circ \mu$ for $\omega\in
    \frakg\dual\otimes \frakg$, extended by the graded Leibniz rule to the rest of
    $\Exter^\bullet(\frakg\dual)\otimes \frakg$. Check the equivalence of the two
    definitions.
\end{exercise}

\begin{example}
    If $V$ is a finite dimensional $\bbk$-vector space, then $\gl(V) =
    \End_\bbk(V)$ is a Lie algebra, isomorphic to $\gl_n(\bbk)$. We omit the
    field from the notation unless necessary, thus simply writing $\gl_n$. 

    Other examples include the Lie algebras 
    \begin{align*}
        \sl_n &= \{X\in\gl_n\;|\; \text{tr}(X)=0\}, \\ 
        \so_n &= \{X\in \gl_n\;|\; X + X^{\sf{T}} = 0\}, \\ 
        \sp_{n} &= \{ X\in \gl_n\;|\; J\, X^{\sf{T}}\, J^{-1} + X = 0\} \qquad
        (n \text{ even, } J\text{ the standard }n\times n\text{ symplectic
        matrix}), \\ 
        \bb_n &= \{X \in \gl_n\;|\; X \text{ upper triangular}\}, \\ 
        \nn_n &= \{X \in \gl_n\;|\; X \text{ strictly upper triangular}\}.
    \end{align*}
\end{example}

\begin{exercise} 
    Check directly that each of the above, equipped with the commutator bracket,
    is a Lie algebra. Which algebraic groups are they the Lie algebras of?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
    Show that $\{\mat{\ast & \ast \\ \ast & \cdot}\}$ is not a Lie subalgebra of
    $\gl_2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{example}
    If $V$ is a $\bbk$-vector space, then the zero form $0\in \Exter^2
    (V\dual)\otimes V$ makes $V$ into a trivial Lie algebra. Such Lie algebras
    are termed \emph{Abelian}. 
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
    Classify one, two, and three-dimensional Lie algebras over $\bbC$.
\end{exercise}

\section{Representations of Lie algebras}

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{representation} of a Lie algebra $\frakg$ on a $\bbk$-vector space $V$ is a
    homomorphism of Lie algebras $\rho \colon \frakg\to \gl(V)$. In this case we
    say ``$\frakg$ acts on $V$''.
\end{definition}

For example, each Lie algebra $\frakg$ acts on itself --- given $x\in \frakg$, define
the map $\adj_x\colon \frakg \to \frakg$ as $y\mapsto [x,y]$.

\begin{lemma}
    The map $\frakg \to \End(\frakg)$ given by $x\mapsto \adj_x$ is a representation of
    $\frakg$. We call this the \emph{adjoint representation}.
    \begin{proof}
        We must show $\adj_{[x,y]} = \adj_x\circ \adj_y - \adj_y\circ \adj_x$.
        But $\adj_{[x,y]}(z)=[[x,y],z]$, while the right-hand side sends $z$ to
        $[x,[y,z]]-[y,[x,z]]$. By antisymmetry, the required result is
        equivalent to the Jacobi identity.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{definition}
    The \emph{center} of $\frakg$ is the kernel of the adjoint action
    $\ker(\adj\colon \frakg\to \gl(\frakg))$
\end{definition}

Thus a Lie algebra with trivial center acts faithfully on itself. 

\begin{remark}
In fact \emph{every} finite dimensional Lie algebra has a faithful finite
dimensional representation. This is a non-trivial theorem of Ado, and the
construction of the representation is non-obvious (unlike the
corresponding statement for algebraic groups, where you can simply consider the
space of functions on the group). We will look at the proof if time permits.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
The Lie algebra $\sl_2=\{\mat{a & b \\ c & -a}\;|\; a,b,c\in \bbk\}$ has a basis given
by the three elements \[e=\mat{0&1\\0&0}, \quad h = \mat{1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1}, \quad f
= \mat{0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0}\]
which satisfy the relations 
\[[h,e]=2e, \quad [h,f]=-2f, \quad [e,f]=h.\]
Hence a representation of $\sl_2$ on $\bbk^n$ is given by a triple $E,F,H\in
\Mat{n}(\bbk)$ such that $EF-FE=H$, $HF-FH=-2F$, and $HE-EH=2E$.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
    If $G$ is an algebraic group, an (algebraic) representation of $G$ on a
    $\bbk$-vector space $V$ is a group homomorphism $\rho\colon G\to
    \sf{GL}(V)$ defined by polynomial equations in matrix coefficients. For the
    ring $\bbE=\bbk[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$, we thus have a map of the
    groups of $\bbE$-points $G(\bbE)\to \sf{GL}(V)(\bbE)$ satisfying
    $\rho(\id)=\id$. It follows that $\rho(\id + X\varepsilon) = \id  +
    d\rho(X)\cdot \varepsilon$ for some matrix $d\rho(X)$. Check that this
    assignment $d\rho\colon \sf{T}_\id G \to \gl(V)$ is a Lie algebra
    homomorphism.
\end{exercise}

\begin{example}
    Take $G=\sf{SL}_2(\bbk)$, and $L(n)=\Sym^n(\bbk^{\oplus 2})$ (equivalently,
    the degree two homogeneous piece of $\bbk[x,y]$). As $\sf{GL}_2(\bbk)$ acts
    on $L(n)$, so does $\sf{SL}_2(\bbk)$. Call this representation $\rho_n\colon
    \sf{SL}(2,\bbk)\to \sf{GL}(L(n))$. For example, $\rho_0$ is the trivial
    representation, $\rho_1$ is the standard representation, and we have \[\rho_2\mat{a&b \\
    c&d} = \mat{a^2 & ab & b^2 \\ 2ac & ad+bc & 2bd \\ c^2 & cd & d^2}.\]
    Differentiating, we get representations $\sl_n\to \gl(L(n))$ which we
    simply refer to as $L(n)$.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
    Writing $(x^iy^j)_{i+j=n}$ for the standard basis of $L(n)$, show that
    $e,f,h\in \sl_2$ act on $L(n)$ as $e (x^iy^j) = jx^{i+1}y^{j-1}$, $f (x^iy^j) = ix^{i-1}y^{j+1}$,
    $h (x^iy^j) = (i-j)x^iy^j$, or equivalently,
    \begin{equation}
        e=x\frac{\partial\phantom{y}}{\partial y}, \quad f = y
        \frac{\partial\phantom{x}}{\partial x}, \quad h =
        x\frac{\partial\phantom{x}}{\partial x} - y
        \frac{\partial\phantom{y}}{\partial y}.\tag{$\ast$}\label{eqn:efh-diffop}
    \end{equation}
    Assuming $\text{char}(\bbk)=0$, show that each $L(n)$ is an irreducible representation.
\end{exercise}

\begin{remark} 
    How to think about these representations $L(n)$? The space $L(n)$ is
    $\HH^0(\bbP^1, \OO(n))$. This is significant because $\bbP^1$ is a
    homogeneous space for the group $\sf{SL}_2$, and $\sl_2$ is precisely the
    Lie algebra of holomorphic vector fields on $\bbP^1$. A second approach to
    defining Lie algebras and their representations is thus to consider
    homogeneous spaces for other algebraic groups.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
    \marginpar{Lecture 3, 13/10/26} 
    Take the group $G=\bbC^\times$ with its lie algebra $\frakg\simeq \bbC$. A
    representation of $\frakg$ on a vector space $V$ is an element $A\in \End(V)$,
    and two such elements $A,A'$ define isomorphic representations if and only
    they are conjugate. Thus the isoclasses of $\frakg$-representations are in
    bijection with Jordan normal forms of matrices. Likewise a subspace
    $W\subset V$ is a subrepresentation if and only if it is preserved by $A$,
    and since any matrix always has an eigenvector, the irreducible (i.e.\ simple)
    $\frakg$-representations are all one-dimensional. In contrast, irreducible
    representations of $G$ are all one-dimensional, but of the form
    $\rho_n\colon z\mapsto z^n$, which have derivative $d\rho_n = n$. Thus out
    of the $\bbC$-many irreducible representations of $\frakg$, only $\bbZ$-many
    come from the Lie group $G$.
\end{example}

The situation for $\sl_2$ is drastically different.

\begin{theorem}[(Lie)]
    If $G$ is a simply connected (almost) simple algebraic group over $\bbC$ (or
    a compact Lie group), with Lie algebra $\frakg$, then the natural map
    $\textnormal{Rep}(G)\to \textnormal{Rep}(\frakg)$ is an equivalence of categories.
\end{theorem}

Here, \emph{almost simple} means that the group has no nontrivial connected
normal subgroup. For example, $\sf{SL}_n(\bbC)$ has centre $\{\pm \id\}$. This
is an important constraint because quotienting by disconnected discrete normal
subgroups does not affect the Lie algebra. The quotient group
$\sf{SL}_n(\bbC)/\{\pm \id\}\simeq \mathsf{PGL}_n(\bbC)$ has the same Lie
algebra as $\sl_2$. This is consistent with the fact that every
$\sf{PGL}_n$-representation is naturally a $\sf{SL}_n$-representation (but this
doesn't account for all $\sf{SL}_n$-representations).

\begin{remark}
    Not all finite dimensional Lie algebras come from Lie groups. In
    characteristic zero, there is a five-dimensional example somewhere in
    Bourbaki. In characteristic $p>0$ it is simpler to see --- noting that the
    $p\times p$ identity matrix is traceless, the quotient $\sl_p/(\bbk\cdot
    \text{id})$ can be shown to not arise from a Lie group.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Classifying representations of $\sl_2(\bbC)$}
 
Let $e,f,h$ be the standard basis of $\sl_2(\bbC)$, with commutation relations
$[e,f]=h$, $[h,e]=2e$, $[h,f]=-2f$.

\begin{theorem}
    Every finite dimensional representation of $\sl_2(\bbC)$ is a direct sum of
    irreducible representations. For all $n\geq 0$, there exists a unique
    irreducible representation of $\sl_2$ that is $(n+1)$-dimensional.
\end{theorem}

We will build up to the proof, by first making some definitions. Thus let $V$ be a
finite dimensional representation of $\sl_2$.

\begin{definition}
    For any complex number $\lambda$, the \emph{$\lambda$-weight space} for $V$
    (denoted $V_\lambda$) is defined as the $\lambda$-eigenspace for the
    operator $h\colon V\to V$.
\end{definition}

Suppose $v\in V_\lambda$. Then the commutation relations imply $ev\in
V_{\lambda+2}$ and $fv\in V_{\lambda-2}$. Thus the actions of $e$ and $f$ move
the vectors `up and down' the $h$-eigenspaces.

\begin{definition}
    If a non-zero vector $v$ lies in $V_\lambda\cap \ker(e)$, i.e.\ satisfies
    $hv=\lambda v$ and $ev = 0$, then we say that $v$ is a \emph{highest weight
    vector of weight $\lambda$}.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
    Let $v\in V_\lambda$ be a highest weight vector of weight $\lambda$. Let
    $W=\langle v, fv, f^2v, \ldots \rangle\subseteq V$. Then $W$ is a
    subrepresentation of $V$.
    \begin{proof}
        We need to show $fW\subseteq W$, $eW\subseteq W$, and $hW\subseteq W$.
        The first and third statements are immediate. To show $eW\subseteq W$,
        show by induction that we have $ef^n v = n(\lambda-n +1)f^{n-1}v$, from
        which the conclusion will follow.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}
    Let $v\in V_\lambda$ be a highest weight vector
    of weight $\lambda$. Then $\lambda \in \bbZ_{\geq 0}$ is a non-negative integer.
    \begin{proof}
        All $f^k v$ ($k\geq 0$) lie in distinct eigenspaces of $h$, so that they
        are linearly independent. Since $V$ is finite dimensional, there is a
        $k$ with $f^kv \neq 0$ but $f^{k+1}v= 0$. But then we have \[0=ef^{k+1}v
        = (k+1)(\lambda-k)f^k v,\]
        and since $(k+1)(f^k v)\neq 0$, we must have $\lambda=k$.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}
    Every finite dimensional representation of $\sl_2(\bbC)$ has a highest
    weight vector.
    \begin{proof}
        Since $V$ is a $\bbC$-vector space, the operator $h$ has an eigenvector
        $v$. Then $v, ev, e^2v, \ldots$ are all vectors which, if non-zero, are
        linearly independent since they lie in distinct eigenspaces of $h$. But
        $V$ is finite dimensional, so there is a $k$ with $e^k v \neq 0$,
        $e^{k+1}v=0$. Then $e^kv$ is a highest weight vector.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{corollary}
    If $V$ is an irreducible representation of $\sl_2(\bbC)$ of dimension $n+1$
    ($n\geq 0$), then $V$ has a basis $v_0,v_1,\ldots, v_n$ with $hv_i =
    (n-2i)v_i$, $fv_i=v_{i+1}$, and $ev_i=i(n-i+1)v_{i-1}$. In particular there
    is a unique $(n+1)$-dimensional representation of $\sl_2(\bbC)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{exercise}
    Write an explicit isomorphism between $L(n)$ and the irreducible
    representation of $\sl_2(\bbC)$ constructed above.
\end{exercise}

We move on to proving \emph{complete reducibility} in $\text{Rep}(\sl_2\bbC)$,
i.e.\ showing that every indecomposable representation is irreducible. When
proving analogous results for finite groups or compact Lie groups, the trick is
to take some average over all group elements. We don't have such tools here.

\begin{definition}
    Let $V$ be a representation of $\sl_2(\bbC)$. The associated \emph{Casimir
    operator}, denoted $\Omega\colon V\to V$, is given by
    $ef+fe+\frac{1}{2}h^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{exercise}
    Show that the operator $\Omega$ is central, i.e.\ it commutes with $e$, $f$
    and $h$. Conclude that if $V$ is an irreducible $\sl_2$-module, then $\Omega$
    acts as a scalar.
    \marginpar{Lecture 4, 15/10/26} 
\end{exercise}

\begin{lemma}
    The Casimir operator of $L(n)$ acts by multiplication by $\frac{1}{2}n^2+n$.
    \begin{proof}
        Note that $\Omega = \frac{1}{2}h^2+h+2fe$, so if $v$ is the highest
        weight vector of $L(n)$, then $\Omega v = (\frac{1}{2}n^2+n)v$. Since
        $f^iv$ ($i\geq 0$) span $L(n)$ and $\Omega f^i = f^i \Omega$, the
        conclusion follows.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{exercise}
    For $\lambda,\mu\in \bbC$, show that $V=x^\lambda y^\mu \,\bbC[\frac{x}{y},
    \frac{y}{x}]$ is a representation of $\sl_2$, where $e,f,h$ act by the
    operators~\eqref{eqn:efh-diffop}. Describe its submodule structure. Write
    down the Casimir operator $\Omega$ as an explicit second-order differential
    operator.
\end{exercise}

Now let $V$ be a finite-dimensional representation of $\sl_2$, and given a
scalar $\lambda$, write $V^\lambda = \ker((\Omega-\lambda)^{\dim V})$ for the
generalised $\lambda$-eigenspace of $\Omega$. Thus each $V^\lambda$ is an
$\sl_2$-submodule of $V$, and we have the decomposition $V=\bigoplus_\lambda
V^\lambda$ as $\sl_2$-modules.

\begin{proposition}[(Vague)]
    \label{prop:casimir-eigenspaces-compseries}
    If $V^\lambda\neq 0$, then $\lambda=\frac{1}{2}n^2+n$ for some integer
    $n\geq 0$, and in this case $V^\lambda$ is `glued together' from copies of $L(n)$. 
\end{proposition}

Now $\Omega$ acts on $L(n)$ by $\frac{1}{2}n^2+n$, which is an increasing
function of $n$ for $n\geq 0$. In particular $\Omega$ acts on $L(n)$ and $L(m)$
with different eigenvalues if $n\neq m$. Thus the above claim makes sense. 

The proposition is vague because we haven't said what `glued together' means; we want
$V^\lambda$ to have a `block upper-triangular form' where diagonal blocks are
$L(n)$. One way to make this precise is to say that $V^\lambda$ has a
\emph{composition series} of $\sl_2$-submodules $0 = W_0 \subseteq W_1 \subseteq
\cdots \subseteq W_r = V^\lambda$ such that the subquotients $W_r/W_{r-1}$ are
all isomorphic to $L(n)$. 

With this interpretation of `glued together', we prove the proposition.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:casimir-eigenspaces-compseries}]
    Since $V^\lambda$ is finite dimensional, it has an irreducible submodule
    $W_1$ isomorphic to $L(n)$ for some $n$. In particular $\Omega$ acts by
    $\frac{1}{2}n^2+n$ on some (and hence all) vectors in $V^\lambda$ so that
    $\lambda=\frac{1}{2}n^2+n$. Now consider $V^\lambda/W_1$. If non-zero, it
    has an irreducible submodule isomorphic to $L(m)$. But $\Omega$
    continues to act on $V^\lambda/W_1$ by $\lambda=\frac{1}{2}n^2+n$ so we must
    have $m=n$. Set $W_2\subset V^\lambda$ to be the preimage of this submodule
    under the quotient $V^\lambda\onto V^\lambda/W_1$, and continue.
\end{proof}

So each finite-dimensional $\sl_2$-module $V$ decomposes as $\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}
V^{(\frac{1}{2}n^2+n)}$ where $V^{(\frac{1}{2}n^2+n)}$ is an iterated
self-extension of $L(n)$s. That is, \emph{strings of different lengths don't
interact.} We must now show that each $V^{(\frac{1}{2}n^2+n)}$ is in fact a
direct sum of copies of $L(n)$s, that is, strings of same length don't interact
either. 

This is subtle, but in a way that we have seen already when we showed that the
eigenvalues of $h$ are quantised.

\begin{proposition}
    The operator $h$ acts on $V^{\frac{1}{2}(n^2+n)}$  with generalised
    eigenvalues in $\{n,n-2,\ldots,2-n, -n\}$.

    \begin{proof}
        We know $h$ acts on $L(n)$ with these eigenvalues. To conclude note that
        the set of generalised eigenvalues of an operator behaves additively on
        short-exact sequences, that is, if $\varphi$ is an endomorphism of a
        vector space $W$ and $W'\subset W$ is a subspace such that
        $\varphi(W')\subseteq W'$, then 
        \[ 
            \{\text{gen.\ eigenvals.\ of }\varphi \text{ on }W\} 
            = \{\text{gen.\ eigenvals.\ of }\varphi \text{ on }W'\} 
            \;\cup\; \{\text{gen.\ eigenvals.\ of }\varphi \text{ on }W/W'\}. 
            \qedhere
        \]
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

Now for $\lambda=\frac{1}{2}n^2+n$, the subspace $V^\lambda\cap
\ker(e)=\ker(e\colon V^\lambda\to V^\lambda)$ is $h$-stable, so the same
observation shows that the only generalised eigenvalue of $h$ on $V^\lambda\cap
\ker(e)$ is $n$. We next show that $h$ in fact acts diagonisably on $V^\lambda
\cap \ker(e)$, so that the adjective `generalised' can be dropped.

\marginpar{Lecture 5, 17/10/26} 
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:h-acts-on-kere}
    The operator $h$ acts as multiplication by $n$ on $V^\lambda\cap \ker(e)$.
    In fact, $x\in V^\lambda$ satisfies $ex=0$ if and only if $hx=nx$, that is,
    $V^\lambda\cap \ker(e)$ is the $n$-eigenspace of $h\colon V^\lambda\to V^\lambda$.
\end{proposition}

To prove this we first recall the following. 

\begin{exercise}
    Prove the following identities of operators.
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item $h f^k = f^k(h-2k)$
        \item $ef^{n+1} = f^{n+1}e + (n+1) f^n (h-n)$
    \end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:h-acts-on-kere}]
    If $hx = nx$, then $hex=(n+2)ex$ and hence $ex=0$ as generalised eigenspaces
    of $h$ on $V^\lambda$ have eigenvalues $-n, \ldots, n$. So $x$ lies in
    $V^\lambda\cap \ker(e)$.

    Conversely let $x\in V^\lambda\cap \ker(e)$, so that in particular $(h-n)^{(\dim V_\lambda)} x
    = 0$. By the above exercise, we have \[0 = f^k(h-n)^{(\dim V_\lambda)} x =
    (h-n+2k)^{(\dim V_\lambda)} f^k x\]
    so that $f^k x$ lies in the $(n-2k)$-generalised eigenspace of $h$.

    Observe that if $y$ is any non-zero element of $\ker(e)$ then $f^n y \neq
    0$ (to see why, let $0=W_0\subseteq \cdots \subseteq  W_r= V^\lambda$ be a composition
    series; there is a unique $i$ with $y\in W_i$ but $y\notin W_{i+1}$. Let
    $\ol{y}=y+W_{i-1}\in W_i/W_{i-1}\simeq L(n)$. Then $\ol y$ is a highest
    weight vector in $L(n)$ so $f^n \ol{y}\neq 0$ in $W_i/W_{i-1}$, from whence
    the conclusion follows.)

    But we have seen that $f^{n+1}x = 0$ (since $x$
    lies in $(-n-2)$-generalised eigenspace) so by the above exercise, 
    \[0=ef^{n+1}x = (n+1)f^n (h-n) x + f^{n+1}ex = (n+1)f^n(h-n)x.\]
    Setting $y=(h-n)x$, we see that $y\in \ker(e)$ (since $e(h-n)x=(h-n-2)ex
    =0$) and $f^ny = f^n(h-n)x = 0$ (since $n+1\neq 0$) and thus $y= 0$.
\end{proof}

Thus $h$ acts diagonally on all of $V^\lambda$.

\begin{exercise}
Choose a basis $w_1,\ldots, w_a$ of $V^\lambda \cap \ker(e)$ and consider the
strings $\langle w_i, fw_i, \ldots, f^n w_i\rangle$, and show that these give a
decomposition of $V^\lambda$ into a direct sum of $L(n)$s. Conclude that finite
dimensional $\sl_2$-modules are completely reducible over $\bbC$, that is, they
are direct sums of irreducible representations.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
    Check that all of this is false over a field of positive characteristic. In
    particular, let $\bbk = \ol{\bbF_p}$ and check the following:
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item the representations $L(n)$ are not completely reducible when $n\geq p$
        \item show that there are irreducible representations with no highest
            weight, and that there are lots such (\emph{hint: show that
            $e^p, f^p, h^p-h$ are all central, so a representation with
            $e^p=\lambda\neq 0$ is not highest weight}.)
    \end{enumerate}
    However, irreducible representations for the algebraic group
    $\sf{SL}_2(\ol{\bbF}_p)$ are parametrised over $n\in \bbZ_{\geq 0}$, and you
    can describe them in terms of $L(n)$. Do so.
\end{exercise}

\subsection{Consequences and character theory}
Let $\frakg$ be a Lie algebra, and $V,W$ be $\frakg$-representations. Then the
map $\frakg\to \End(V\otimes W)$ given by $x\mapsto x\otimes 1 + 1\otimes x$
defines a homomorphism of Lie algebras, so that $V\otimes W$ is naturally a
$\frakg$-representation.

\begin{remark}
    This is related to the fact that both group algebras and enveloping algebras
    are \emph{Hopf algebras}. In general if $V,W$ are $A$-modules then $V\otimes
    W$ is an $(A\otimes A)$-module, so to turn it into an $A$-module you need a
    homomorphism $A\to (A\otimes A)$.
\end{remark}

For $\frakg = \sl_2(\bbC)$, we see that $L(n)\otimes L(m)$ is thus a representation of
$\sl_2$. What is its decomposition into irreducibles? We could just find all
highest weight vectors, indeed you should do this by hand for small examples
such as $L(1)\otimes L(n)$ and $L(2)\otimes L(n)$. For example, it is easy to
see that if $v_n\in L(n)$ and $v_m\in L(m)$ are highest weight vectors, then
check that so is $v_n\otimes v_m \in L(n)\otimes L(m)$. Thus $L(n)\otimes L(m) =
L(n+m) \oplus \text{(other factors)}$. The other factors must account for $(n+1)(m+1)-(n+m+1)$ dimensions. 

Characters provide a convenient way to calculate what such decompositions are in
general.

\begin{definition}
    Let $V$ be a finite dimensional representation of $\sl_2$. The
    \emph{(formal) character} of $V$ is the Laurent polynomial
    \[\char V=\sum_{n\in \bbZ} (\dim V_n) z^n \qquad \in \bbZ_{\geq 0
    }[z,z^{-1}].\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    The character of $V$ has the following properties. 
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item The character is independent of choice of basis for $\sl_2$.
        \item We have $\char V|_{z=1}=\dim V$.
            \begin{proof} This is precisely the claim that $h$ acts diagonisably
            on $V$.\end{proof}
        \item $\char\, L(n) = z^n + z^{n-2} + \cdots + z^{2-n} + z^{-n}.$
        \item $\char V = \char W$ if and only if $V\simeq W.$
            \begin{proof}
                Write $V\simeq \bigoplus a_n L(n)$, $W\simeq b_n L(n)$. Then
                $V\simeq W$ if and only if $a_n = b_n$ for all $n\geq 0$ by our
                classification of $\sl_2$-modules. But note that $\{\char
                L(n)\;|\; n\geq 0\}$ is a basis of the space
                $\bbZ[z,z^{-1}]^{\text{sym}}$ of symmetric polynomials, so the
                $a_n$s are determined by $\char V$.
            \end{proof}

        \item $\char (V\otimes W) = (\char V )\cdot (\char W).$
            \begin{proof}
                Note that $V_a\otimes W_b \subseteq (V\otimes W)_{a+b}$, so
                that $(V\otimes W)_r = \sum_{a+b = r}V_a\otimes V_b$. But this
                is precisely how we multiply polynomials.
            \end{proof}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The character of $L(n)$, i.e.\ the polynomial $[n+1]_z\coloneqq z^n + z^{n-2} +
\cdots + z^{2-n} + z^{-n}$ is also sometimes called the $(n+1)$th \emph{quantum
integer}.

\begin{example}
    What is $L(1)\otimes L(3)$? Explicitly multiplying polynomials, we
    compute that $[2]_z \cdot [4]_z = [3]_z + [5]_z$, and hence $L(1)\otimes
    L(3)\simeq L(2)\oplus L(4)$. More generally, we have the
    \emph{Clebsch--Gordon rule}, which says that $L(n)\otimes L(m)\simeq

\section{Structure of complex semisimple Lie algebras}
\marginpar{Lecture 6, 20/10/26} 
\begin{definition}
    A Lie algebra $\frakg$ is \emph{simple} if it is not Abelian and its only
    ideals are $0$ and $\frakg$. A Lie algebra $\frakg$ is \emph{semisimple} if it is
    a direct sum of simple ones.
\end{definition}

We would like to describe how Lie algebras are built out of simple and
semisimple ones. For this, more definitions are in order.

\begin{definition}
    Given a Lie algebra $\frakg$, its \emph{derived subalgebra}
    $[\frakg,\frakg]$ (also denoted $\frakg^1$ or $\frakg^{(1)}$) is the span of
    all elements $[x,y]$ where $x,y\in \frakg$. More generally, the \emph{central series} of $\frakg$ is the sequence of ideals
    $\frakg^n\subseteq \frakg$ ($n\geq 0$) defined recursively as
    \[\frakg^0=\frakg, \qquad \frakg^n = [\frakg^{n-1}, \frakg] =
    \text{span}\{\,[x,y]\;|\; x\in \frakg^{n-1}, y\in\frakg\}.\] 
    On the other hand, the \emph{derived} (or \emph{lower central}) series of
    $\frakg$ is the sequence of ideals $\frakg^{(n)}\subseteq \frakg$ ($n\geq
    0$) defined recursively as
    \[
        \frakg^{(0)}=\frakg, \qquad \frakg^{(n)} = [\frakg^{(n-1)},
        \frakg^{(n-1)}] = \text{span}\{\,[x,y]\;|\; x\in \frakg^{(n-1)}, y\in
        \frakg^{(n-1)}\}.
    \] 
    We say $\frakg$ is \emph{nilpotent} if $\frakg^n=0$ for $n\gg 0$ (i.e.\ the
    ``central series terminates''), and $\frakg$ is \emph{solvable} if $\frakg^{(n)}=0$
    for $n\gg 0$ (i.e.\ the ``derived series terminates'').
\end{definition}

It is clear that $\frakg^{(n)}\subseteq \frakg^n$ for all $n$, and hence every
nilpotent Lie algebra is solvable. 

\begin{example}
    The two dimensional Lie algebra with basis $x,y$ such that $[x,y]=y$ is
    solvable but not nilpotent. Note this is a subalgebra of $\bb_2=\{\mat{\ast
    & \ast \\ 0 &\ast}\}$, the Lie
    algebra of $2\times 2$ upper triangular matrices. More generally, the Lie
    algebra $\bb_n\subset \gl_n$ of $n\times n$ upper triangular matrices is
    solvable but not nilpotent. The algebra $\nn_n$ of \emph{strictly} upper
    triangular matrices is on the other hand nilpotent.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
    Compute the central and derived series of $\bb_n$ and $\nn_n$ to check the
    above claims. Compute the centres of these algebras.
\end{exercise}

\begin{example}
    Let $(W, \langle -, -\rangle)$ be a symplectic vector space. The
    \emph{Heisenberg Lie algebra} $\mathcal{H}_W$ has underlying vector space
    $W\oplus \bbk \, c$, with the Lie bracket given by \([c,w]=0\) and
    $[w,w']=\langle w, w'\rangle c$ for all $w,w'\in W$. This is a nilpotent Lie
    algebra.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
    Let $W$ be the standard two-dimensional symplectic space, and
    $L\subset W$ a Lagrangian subspace. Then $W$ is isomorphic to $L\oplus
    L\dual$ with symplectic form $\langle L, L\rangle = \langle L\dual,
    L\dual\rangle = 0$, and for $\lambda \in L\dual$, $\ell \in
    L$ we have $\langle \lambda , \ell\rangle = \lambda(\ell)$. Choose a basis
    $p,q$ of $W$ corresponding to dual bases of $L,L\dual$, so that $p,q,c$ is a
    basis of the Heisenberg Lie algebra $\mathcal{H}_W$. Show this has a
    representation on $\bbC[x]$ where $q$ acts by multiplication by $x$, $p$
    acts by $\frac{\partial\phantom{x}}{\partial x}$, and $c$ acts as the identity.
\end{exercise}

We mention equivalent characterisations of solvability and nilpotency, which are
straightforward to prove.

\begin{proposition}
    Subalgebras and quotient algebras of solvable Lie algebras are
    solvable. In fact, if $\frakg$ is a Lie algebra and
    $\frakh\subset \frakg$ is an ideal, then $\frakg$ is solvable if and
    only if $\frakh$ and $\frakg/\frakh$ are both solvable. 

    Consequently, $\frakg$ is solvable if and only if there is a sequence of
    subalgebras $0=\frakh_1\subseteq \cdots\subseteq \frakh_n=\frakg$ such that
    each $\frakh_i$ is an ideal of $\frakh_{i+1}$, and $\frakh_{i+1}/\frakh_i$
    is Abelian.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}
Subalgebras and quotient algebras of nilpotent Lie algebras are nilpotent. In
fact a Lie algebra $\frakg$ is nilpotent if and only if its center $Z(\frakg)$
is non-zero, and the quotient $\frakg/Z(\frakg)$ is nilpotent.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Three theorems} The theorems of Lie, Engel, and Cartan
form the cornerstones of the study of nilpotency and solvability in Lie algebras.

\begin{theorem}[(Lie's theorem)]
    Let $\bbk$ be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, and
    $\frakg$ a solvable Lie algebra over $\bbk$. If $V$ is a finite-dimensional
    $\frakg$-module, then there is a basis $v_1,\ldots, v_n$ of $V$
    with respect to which all elements of $\frakg$ are upper triangular.
    That is, there is a chain of $\frakg$-modules $0=V_0\subseteq V_1\subseteq
    \cdots \subseteq V_n = V$ with each $V_i$ being $i$-dimensional (such a
    chain is called a \emph{complete flag}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{exercise}
    Show that Lie's theorem is equivalent to the statement that given any solvable
    Lie algebra $\frakg$ over an algebraically closed field of characteristic
    zero, every $\frakg$-module has a one-dimensional submodule. 
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
    Show that Lie's theorem can fail over fields that aren't algebraically
    closed or of positive characteristic (\emph{hint: for the latter, consider
    the three-dimensional Heisenberg algebra over a field $\bbk$ of
    characteristic $p$, and consider the representation $\bbk[x]/(x^p)$.})
\end{exercise}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:derived-of-solvable-is-nilp}
    Over a field of characteristic zero, the derived subalgebra of a solvable
    Lie algebra is nilpotent.
\end{corollary}

\begin{exercise}
    Show that for a Lie algebra $\frakg$ over $\bbk$, the base extension to the
    algebraic closure $\frakg\otimes_\bbk \ol{k}$ is solvable if and only if
    $\frakg$ is.
\end{exercise}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{cor:derived-of-solvable-is-nilp}]
        Given the exercise above, we can assume $\bbk$ is algebraically closed,
        and apply Lie's theorem to the adjoint representation
        $\adj(\frakg)\subseteq \End(\frakg)$. Since $\frakg$ is solvable, so is
        $\adj(\frakg)$ and hence $\adj(\frakg)$ lies in the Borel subalgebra
        $\bb\subseteq \End(\frakg)$ by Lie's theorem. But a direct computation
        shows that $[\bb, \bb]$ is nilpotent, so that $[\adj(\frakg),
        \adj(\frakg)]$ is so too. Since $[\adj(\frakg), \adj(\frakg)] =
        \adj[\frakg, \frakg]$, we conclude that $[\frakg, \frakg]$ is itself
        nilpotent by the criterion above.
\end{proof}

\begin{exercise}
    Show that~\cref{cor:derived-of-solvable-is-nilp} fails in positive characteristic.
\end{exercise}

\begin{theorem}[(Engel's theorem)] 
    \marginpar{Lecture 7, 22/10/26} Over an arbitrary field $\bbk$, a Lie
    algebra $\frakg$ is nilpotent if and only if every finite-dimensional
    $\frakg$-module $V$, on which every $x\in \frakg$ acts nilpotently, has a
    basis $v_1,\ldots, v_n$ with respect to which $\frakg$ acts by strictly
    upper triangular matrices (that is, all operators in $\frakg$ are
    simultaneously strictly-upper-triangularisable on $V$).
\end{theorem}

Engel's theorem says that a non-nilpotent Lie algebra cannot act exclusively by
nilpotent operators. The converse is false! Indeed, the Lie algebra of diagonal
$n\times n$ matrices is Abelian and hence nilpotent, but it doesn't act by
nilpotent matrices on $\bbk^n$. The `correct' converse is that if $\frakg$ is a
nilpotent Lie algebra, then $\frakg/Z(\frakg)$ always acts by nilpotent matrices.

\begin{exercise}
    Show that Engel's theorem is equivalent to the statement that given a
    nilpotent Lie algebra $\frakg$, every finite-dimensional $\frakg$-module $V$
    has a one-dimensional trivial submodule (i.e.\ a subspace $\bbk v\subset V$
    such that $xv=0$ for each $x\in \frakg$).
\end{exercise}

\begin{corollary}
    A Lie algebra $\frakg$ is nilpotent if and only if each operator
    $\adj_x\colon \frakg\to \frakg$ ($x\in \frakg$) is nilpotent.
    \begin{proof}
        Given $x,y\in \frakg$, note that $\adj_x^i(y)$ lies in $\frakg^i$ and
        hence if $\frakg$ is nilpotent then $\adj_x$ is a nilpotent operator.
        Conversely if each $\adj_x$ is a nilpotent operator, then by Engel's
        theorem, $\frakg$ embeds as a subalgebra in the Lie algebra $\nn_n$
        of strictly upper triangular $n\times n$ matrices. It follows that
        $\frakg^{n}\subseteq (\nn_n)^{n}=0$.
    \end{proof}
\end{corollary}

To prove Engel's theorem we make a few definitions. A symmetric bilinear form
$(-,-)\colon \frakg\times\frakg\to \bbk$ is \emph{invariant} if
$([x,y],z)=(x,[y,z])$. For example, each finite dimensional representation $V$
of $\frakg$ defines a symmetric bilinear form $(x,y)\mapsto \text{trace}(xy\colon V\to V)$, and this is invariant.

\begin{exercise}
    Suppose $\frakg$ is the Lie algebra of a Lie group $G$. Check that a
    $G$-invariant symmetric bilinear form $(-,-)\colon \frakg\times \frakg\to
    \frakg$ is $\frakg$-invariant, by differentiating the relation $(gx,
    gy)=(x,y)$ over $g\in G$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
    If $(-,-)$ is an invariant form on $\frakg$ and $\fraka\subseteq \frakg$ is
    an ideal, then so is $\fraka\orth$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{definition}
    The \emph{Killing form} of a finite dimensional Lie algebra
    $\frakg$ is the trace form of its adjoint representation, given by
    $(x,y)_\adj = \text{trace}(\adj_x\circ \adj_y\colon\frakg \to \frakg)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[(Cartan's criterion)]
    Let $\frakg$ be a finite dimensional Lie algebra over a field of
    characteristic $0$, and $V$ a faithful finite-dimensional representation.
    Then $\frakg$ is solvable if and only if the derived subalgebra
    $\frakg^{(1)}$ lies in the kernel of the associated trace form, i.e.\
    $\textnormal{trace}(xyz-xzy\colon V\to V)=0$ for all $x,y,z\in
    \frakg$.

%        so that the vanishing of $\textnormal{trace}(xyz-xzy\colon V\to V)$ can
%        be checked explicitly.

\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}
    Over a field of characteristic zero, a Lie algebra $\frakg$ is solvable if
    and only $(\frakg, [\frakg, \frakg])_\adj=0$.
    \begin{proof}
        Note that $\frakg$ is solvable if and only if its quotient
        $\adj(\frakg)=\frakg/Z(\frakg)$ is (since the center $Z(\frakg)$ is Abelian and
        hence solvable). But the adjoint representation $\frakg\circlearrowright
        \frakg$ induces a faithful representation $\adj(\frakg)\circlearrowright
        \frakg$, so we can conclude by Cartan's criterion.
    \end{proof}
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Criteria for semisimplicity} As an application of the above
theorems, we prove a powerful criterion for checking that a Lie algebra is
semisimple. The proof is largely bookkeeping.

\begin{exercise}
    Show that if $\fraka_1, \fraka_2\subseteq \frakg$ are solvable ideals, then
    so is $\fraka_1+\fraka_2$. Conclude that every finite-dimensional Lie
    algebra has a maximal solvable ideal $R(\frakg)$, which we call its \emph{radical}.
    Check that $R(\frakg/R(\frakg))=0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{theorem}
    If $\frakg$ is a finite dimensional Lie algebra over a field of
    characteristic zero, the following are equivalent.
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item\label{item:killing1} The algebra $\frakg$ is semisimple.
        \item\label{item:killing2} The radical $R(\frakg)$ is zero. 
        \item\label{item:killing3} The algebra $\frakg$ has no non-zero Abelian ideals.
        \item\label{item:killing4} \emph{(Killing criterion)} The Killing form
            $(-,-)_\adj\colon \frakg\times \frakg\to \frakg$ is non-degenerate.
    \end{enumerate}

    \begin{proof}
        If $\fraka\subset\frakg$ is an Abelian ideal, then $\fraka$ lies in
        $\frakg\orth=\{x\in \frakg\;|\; (x,y)_\adj = 0 \text{ for all }y\in
        \frakg\}$. Indeed, we can extend a basis $v_1,\ldots, v_m$ of $\fraka$
        to a basis $v_1,\ldots, v_n$ of $\frakg$, and note that with respect to
        this basis any element $x\in \frakg$ act by a matrix of the block-form
        $\mat{\ast & \ast \\ 0 & \ast}$ while any $a\in \fraka$ act by
        a matrix of the form $\mat{0 & \ast \\ 0 & 0}$, from which it follows that
        $\adj_x\circ \adj_a$ is traceless. Thus~\ref{item:killing4} implies~\ref{item:killing3}.

        The implication~\ref{item:killing3}$\Rightarrow$\ref{item:killing2}
        follows by noting that the final non-zero term in the derived series of
        $R(\frakg)$ is necessarily Abelian, and an ideal of $\frakg$ (to see
        this, note that the Jacobi identity shows if $\frakh_1,\frakh_2\subseteq
        \frakg$ are ideals then so is $[\frakh_1,\frakh_2]$).

        To show~\ref{item:killing2}$\Rightarrow$\ref{item:killing4}, consider
        the ideal $\frakg\orth\subseteq \frakg$. If $R(\frakg)=0$, then in
        particular $\frakg$ has trivial centre and hence we have embeddings
        $\frakg \orth\subseteq \frakg\subseteq \End(\frakg)$. In particular,
        $\frakg \orth$ acts faithfully on $\frakg$ and the associated trace form
        (the restriction of the Killing form) is trivial by definition of
        $\frakg\orth$. Thus Cartan's criterion shows $\frakg\orth$ is solvable,
        hence lies in $R(\frakg)=0$. That is, $\frakg\orth=0$ as required. 

        Now suppose the equivalent conditions~\ref{item:killing2}--\ref{item:killing4} hold, so that the Killing form is
        non-degenerate and $\frakg$ has no non-zero solvable ideals. For any
        minimal non-zero ideal $\frakh\subset \frakg$, we then see that
        the restricted Killing form $(-,-)_\adj|_{\frakh}$ is non-degenerate ---
        indeed, the kernel of this restricted form is the ideal $\frakh \orth
        \cap \frakh$ of $\frakg$
        contained in $\frakh$, so is zero or $\frakh$ by minimality, but if we had
        $\frakh\orth\cap \frakh = \frakh$ then that would imply solvability of
        $\frakh$ by Cartan's criterion. Thus $\frakh\orth$ and $\frakh$ are
        ideals of $\frakg$ that intersect trivially, giving the decomposition
        $\frakg=\frakh\orth \oplus \frakh$ as Lie algebras. It follows that
        ideals of $\frakh$ (and $\frakh\orth$) are also ideals of $\frakg$, so
        that $\frakh$ is simple and $\frakh\orth$ has trivial radical allowing
        us to continue and inductively decompose $\frakg$ into a direct-sum of
        its minimal ideals showing~\ref{item:killing1}. The converse implication~\ref{item:killing1}$\Rightarrow$\ref{item:killing3} is obvious.
    \end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{exercise}
    Show that the chain of containments $[R(\frakg),R(\frakg)]\subseteq
    \frakg\orth\subseteq R(\frakg)\subseteq \frakg$ holds in general.
\end{exercise}

\end{exercise}

We note that the above theorem shows every finite dimensional Lie algebra $\frakg$ in
characteristic zero is the extension of a semisimple Lie algebra
(namely $\frakg/R(\frakg)$) by a solvable one (namely $R(\frakg)$).

\begin{exercise}
    Let $\frakg\subset \gl_n$ be the Lie subalgebra of matrices $X$ for which
    the lower right $m\times (n-m)$ block $[X_{ij}\;|\; m+1 \leq i\leq n,\,1\leq
    j\leq m]$ vanishes. Write the associated sequence $0\to R(\frakg)\to \frakg \to
    \frakg/R(\frakg)\to 0$, and shoe explicitly that it splits (i.e.\ that
    $\frakg$ has a subalgebra $\mathfrak{s}$ isomorphic to $\frakg/R(\frakg)$,
    so that $\frakg \simeq \mathfrak{s}\ltimes R(\frakg)$). Check that this
    splitting is non-unique.
\end{exercise}

The above phenomenon is more general.

\begin{theorem}[(Levi's theorem)]
    If $\frakg$ is a finite dimensional Lie algebra over a field of
    characteristic zero, then the radical sequence $0\to R(\frakg)\to \frakg \to
    \frakg/R(\frakg)\to 0$ splits.
\end{theorem}

Levi's theorem is, of course, false over a field of positive characteristic.

\begin{exercise}
    Show that the radical of $\sl_p(\bbF_p)$ is $\bbk\cdot \id$, and that
    $\sl_p(\bbF_p)/\bbk\cdot \id$ does not embed into $\sl_p(\bbF_p)$. 
\end{exercise}

\subsection{Derivations} A \emph{derivation} of a Lie algebra $\frakg$ is a
linear map $D\colon\frakg\to \frakg$ such that $D[x,y]=[Dx,y]+[x,Dy]$ for all
$x,y\in \frakg$. For
example, each $x\in \frakg$ induces a derivation $\adj_x\colon \frakg\to
\frakg$; such derivations are called \emph{inner derivations}. The vector space
of all derivations up to inner derivations is denoted $\HH^1(\frakg, \frakg)$,
the first cohomology of $\frakg$ with coefficients in $\frakg$. 

We will learn more about Lie algebra cohomology in due time.

\begin{exercise}
    If $D\colon \frakg\to \frakg$ is a derivation and $x\in \frakg$, then show
    that $\adj_{Dx}=[D,\adj_x]\in \End(\frakg)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{proposition}
    If $\frakg$ is a semisimple Lie algebra, then $\HH^1(\frakg,\frakg)=0$
    (i.e.\ all derivations of $\frakg$ are inner).

    \begin{proof}
        Given a derivation $D\colon \frakg\to \frakg$ of a semisimple Lie
        algebra, consider the linear function $\frakg\to \bbk$ given by
        $x\mapsto \text{trace}(D\circ \adj_x\colon \frakg\to \frakg)$. Since the
        Killing form is non-degenerate, this element of $\frakg\dual$ is equal to
        $(y,-)_\adj$ for a unique $y\in \frakg$. Claim $D=\adj_y$.

        To show this, we show $E=D-\adj_y$ is zero, or equivalently $Ex=0$ for
        all $x\in \frakg$. By non-degeneracy of the Killing form, it suffices to
        show $(Ex,z)_\adj$ vanishes for
        all $x,z\in \frakg$. But this is immediate, since
        \begin{align*}
            (Ex,z)_\adj 
            &= \textnormal{trace}(\adj_{Ex}\circ \adj_z) \\
            &=\textnormal{trace}([E,\adj_x]\circ \adj_z) \\
            &= \textnormal{trace}(E\circ [\adj_x, \adj_z]) \\ 
            &= \underbrace{\textnormal{trace}(D\circ [\adj_x,
            \adj_z])}_{(y,[x,z])_\adj} -
            \underbrace{\textnormal{trace}(\adj_y \circ
            [\adj_x,\adj_z])}_{(y,[x,z])_{\adj}} \qquad = 0. \qedhere
        \end{align*}
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

    $\HH^1(\frakg,\frakg)$ is \emph{not} a criterion for semisimplicity.
\end{exercise}

\section{Structure of complex simple Lie algebras}
The punchline of this section is a precise description of how algebras such as
$\sl_n(\bbC)$ are built out of $\sl_2(\bbC)$. 

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{torus} $\tt\subset\frakg$ is an Abelian Lie subalgebra such that for
    all $t\in \tt$, the operator $\adj_t\colon \frakg\to \frakg$ is
    diagonalisable. A \emph{maximal torus} is a torus not contained in a bigger
    torus (duh).
\end{definition}

\begin{exercise}

    $\gl_n$ (resp.\ $\sl_n$).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
    Check that the Abelian Lie subalgebra $\mat{0&\ast \\ 0&0}\subseteq \sl_2$
    is not a torus.
\end{exercise}

If $V$ is a $\bbC$-vector space and $t_1,\ldots,t_n\colon V\to V$ are diagonalisable
and pairwise commuting linear maps, then the \emph{simultaneous eigenspace}
associated to a tuple $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)\in \bbC^n$ is the
subspace 
\[V_{\lambda}=\{v\in V\;|\; t_i v = \lambda_i v \text{ for all }i\}.\]
Since commuting diagonalisable operators are simultaneously diagonalisable, we
have $V=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in \bbC^n}V_{\lambda}$.

The above calculation can be rephrased by saying that irreducible
$\bbC$-representations of an Abelian Lie algebra $\tt$ are all one-dimensional,
and are parametrised over the dual space $\tt\dual$. Elements of $\tt\dual$ are
called \emph{weights}. If $V$ is a $\tt$-module on which every $t\in \tt$ acts
diagonalisably, the decomposition $V=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\tt\dual}V_\lambda$ of
a $\tt$-module $V$ into simultaneous eigenspaces for elements of $\tt$ is called
a \emph{weight-space decomposition}. If $V_\lambda\neq 0$ in this decomposition, we say that $\lambda$
is a \emph{weight of $V$}.

Applying the above to a maximal torus $\tt$ in a Lie algebra $\frakg$, we obtain
a weight space decomposition $\frakg=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in \tt\dual}
\frakg_\lambda$. Evidently $\tt\subseteq \frakg_0$, so $0$ is a weight of
$\frakg$. The non-zero weights of $\frakg$ are called the \emph{roots} of
$\frakg$.

\begin{example}
    Suppose $\frakg=\sl_n(\bbC)$, and $\tt$ is the torus of diagonal matrices. Note
    $\frakg = \tt \,\oplus\, \bigoplus_{i\neq j}\bbk E_{ij}$, where $E_{ij}$ is the
    matrix whose $(i,j)$th entry is $1$, and the remaining entries are $0$. If
    $t\in \tt$ is the matrix $\text{diag}(t_1,\ldots,t_n)$, then we compute
    $[t,E_{ij}]=(t_i-t_j)E_{ij}$. Thus if $\varepsilon_i\in \tt\dual$ is the
    weight $\text{diag}(t_1,\ldots, t_n)\mapsto t_i$, then we have shown that 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item[] the roots of $\frakg$ are $\varepsilon_i-\varepsilon_j$ for
            $i\neq j$,
        \item[] the weight space $\frakg_{(\varepsilon_i-\varepsilon_j)}$ is
            one-dimensional and spanned by $E_{ij}$, and
        \item[] the weight space $\frakg_0$ is the torus $\tt$ itself (which is
            therefore, in particular, maximal).
    \end{enumerate}
    Note that the weights $\varepsilon_1,\ldots, \varepsilon_n$ are not linearly
    independent, but satisfy the relation $\varepsilon_1+\cdots +
    \varepsilon_n=0$.
\end{example}
